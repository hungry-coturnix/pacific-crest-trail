# Cow Canyon to Walker Pass

May 30, 2026 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Southern California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/southern-california/)

**Start:** [35.737697, -118.077667](https://maps.app.goo.gl/1WCgXZ4PhbzDV1tVA) (CA-178 at the mouth of Cow Canyon)  
**End:** [35.662919, -118.026847](https://maps.app.goo.gl/qoXghZhHqLCHAJhQ8) (Walker Pass Trailhead)

This is a reverse hike to reduce elevation gain on the way back.

**Distance:** 17.3 mi (up to PCT mile 665.9, 25.1% completed)  
**Time:** 8 hours 48 minutes (start 6:24am, end 3:13pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 5,176 ft (low 3,821 ft – high 7,350 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 3,734 ft (start 3,833 ft – end 5,275 ft)

---

My typical PCT schedule is to go on another hike every other weekend. But somehow I was really looking forward to another PCT adventure for the third consecutive weekend. It was also going to help with the heat. The further north I got the better, the higher elevations would make hiking more enjoyable. The next 50 miles were still considered to be desert but past Kennedy Meadows South (KMS) I would enter the High Sierra.

Ahead was the California Section G of the Pacific Crest Trail. Turning this stretch into appropriate day hike slices had required some thought. My plan was to hike up Cow Canyon using an abandoned dirt road which the Civilian Conservation Corps crews had used to build this section of the PCT. The dirt road was marked as unmaintained on all maps, but satellite images showed a pretty clear path. The only challenge was at the very beginning, where a private property was perched between the highway and the dirt road. I would have to do a bit of cross-country hiking to get around the property and to the dirt road. After that, the road entered Owens Peak Wilderness, which meant public land.

I spent the night at Walker Pass Campground, a first-come, first-served BLM managed campground with no fees. Arriving at 8pm sharp, the sun had just set. Several car spots were already taken and a few thru hikers were also camping here. Fortunately, there was a very flat gravel spot along the access road, just 50 yards from the restrooms. I quickly set up the rooftop tent and settled in for the night. The highway was not far and I could hear the cars making their way up the pass but it was infrequent enough to not disturb me. The clouds in the distance were orange and red and there was no wind. Overall, it was very peaceful.

Temperatures at night dropped all the way to 34°F / 1°C and I could feel the chill in the very early morning hours. My alarm went off at 5:15am. It was still 23 minutes to sunrise, but there was already plenty of light. I quietly packed my stuff. The tent had collected quite a bit of dew. My first stop was Walker Pass, just 2 minutes away. I chained my bike to a signpost 20 yards up the trail. Then it was about 7 miles to a dirt pullout along the highway just opposite the private property. I geared up and started hiking at 6:24am. The private property had a barbed wire fence which I followed north through willows. At the end of the fence, I cut around crossing a shallow creek, then made my way up 20 yards to find the dirt road. So far so good. The dirt road was still in pretty decent condition and fairly easy to follow. The elevation gain was immanent and steady though.

As I made my way up Cow Canyon, I kept turning around to enjoy the view. The sun had come over the mountain ridge to the east and was slowly painting the mountains to the west in golden hues. Plenty of Joshua trees dotted the canyon bottom. About halfway up the canyon, the dirt road crossed a creek which I managed without getting my feet wet. After almost 3 miles, the dirt road disappeared, but fortunately, I was only a few hundred yards away from the PCT. With some help of my offline map I soon emerged onto the trail and headed south. After a short dip, the trail continued my steady ascent further up Cow Canyon. A little over half a mile later, I reached a junction with a short side trail to Joshua Tree Spring.

Side story: A few months ago, my donations to the Pacific Crest Trail Association, the non-profit organization which stewards the trail, had turned into a series of phone calls and virtual meetings about how I might be able to contribute to the trail through data collection and photos. As a result, I pledged to spend some time during my hikes to collect data about and take photos of every water source and trailhead. On top of that, I was also going to donate my best landscape photos from the hike to the PCTA’s [Explore](https://explore.pcta.org/) website dedicated to making the trail more accessible and recommending possible day hike options. I am incredibly excited about this endeavor as it combines three of my passions: hiking, photography, and data.

Joshua Tree Spring was a quarter mile off the PCT down a fairly steep trail. The upper part of the trail had great views of Cow Canyon. The spring itself was nestled among plenty of trees in a shady spot. The American Hiking Society had built a very large concrete trough with a metal pipe here. There was a very strong water flow. The trough was hosting a lively population of water striders. I took my first break here. I had brought enough water, the spring was still very nice though. Making my way back to the PCT, I continued the ascent. The trail was just west of Owens Peak and steadily gaining elevation. The views down Cow Canyon and beyond were stunning. With a few switchbacks, the trail made it up a saddle just north of Berts Canyon, only to continue the ascent and reach another, higher saddle south of Owens Peak. I took another break just before reaching the second saddle, enjoying the views down Berts Canyon.

Past the saddle, the elevation gain was much more modest. The views were breathtaking though. To the north was Owens Peak with a long exposed granite ridge heading east, forming the norther wall of Indian Wells Canyon. The canyon in itself was stunning, I could see it reach the desert in the distance. More and more northbound thru hikers were passing me now. The trail clung to the eastern side of the ridge with Indian Wells Canyon to my left. One hiker was jokingly telling me that I was walking in the wrong direction. After about 11 miles, I reached the east side of Mount Jenkins. My map showed a spur trail up to the summit that I wanted to take. This was not an official trail though. In fact it was barely noticeable as a trail if you didn’t know where it was. And it was incredibly steep. After slowly making my way up for about a quarter mile, I gave up. It was too steep and too much loose rock. I took another break enjoying the views east, then carefully made my way back to the PCT.

From now on, it was mostly downhill. The trail headed for another saddle just north of Morris Peak with a spectacular canyon on either side, then rounded Morris Peak. To the west were stunning views of Three Pines Canyon. After staying mostly on the west side of the ridge, the PCT eventually moved back to the eastern side, revealing another great view down Freeman Canyon where I could see the highway. After a couple switchbacks, the trail rounded the knob just north of Walker Pass, the finally reached the pass itself. It had gradually become warmer now, but a light breeze had kept it manageable.

---

**Trail Conditions:** Excellent dirt singletrack interspersed with patches of heavy, loose rock. The lower basin of Indian Wells Canyon and the area east of Mount Jenkins partially burned during the 2010 Indian Fire. Fortunately, the fire did not consume the entire pinyon pine and juniper belt at the higher elevations.

**Parking:** There is room for a few cars in a large pullout along Highway 178.

**Crowds:** Lightly traveled. Met 38 northbound thru hikers throughout the day.

**Weather:** Sunny with no clouds. Light to moderate intermittent breeze past noon. Low of 49°F / 9°C at the beginning of the hike in Cow Canyon. High of 81°F / 27°C in the afternoon on the exposed and bare hillsides just north of Walker Pass. Humidity between 70% in the morning and 29% in the afternoon.

**Land Manager & Permits:** Owens Peak Wilderness. No permit required.

**Overall Rating:** 4.4

Difficulty: Strenuous  
Scenery: Superb  
Logistics: Very Easy

---

**Bike Shuttle:** 6.7 mi (19 minutes), 0 ft elevation gain, 20.71 mph average speed, 38.57 mph maximum speed

The bike ride back was one of the easiest in a while. It was all downhill from Walker Pass albeit with significant headwind which meant that I didn’t have to use my brakes much. Traffic was very light and I was back at my car in no time.
