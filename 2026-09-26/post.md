Cottonwood Pass to Bullfrog Lake Trail (Day 1)

Start: 36.448336, -118.170638 (Cottonwood Pass Trailhead)
End: 36.551884, -118.358122 (Lower Crabtree Meadow)

Distance: 21.2 mi / 34.1 km (up to PCT mile 767.5, 29.0% completed)
Time: 11 hours 17 minutes (start 6:41am, end 5:59pm)
Elevation Gain: 4,166 ft / 1,270 m (low 9,519 ft / 2,901 m – high 11,507 ft / 3,507 m)
Elevation Loss: 3,768 ft / 1,149 m (start 9,936 ft / 3,028 m – end 10,334 ft / 3,150 m)

----------
Back from my two-week vacation in the Wallowa Mountains in Oregon, my goal was to get as far north on the Pacific Crest Trail as weather conditions would allow. It was unpredictable when the first snow would arrive and even without snow, I wasn't sure how cold it would get at night high up in the mountains. Unlike the previous two years hiking through the lower desert ranges of Southern California, I would definitely have to stop over the winter while the High Sierra passes along the PCT were buried under deep snow.

The next section was going to be a three-day backpacking trip, starting at Cottonwood Pass Trailhead for the third and last time, then rejoining the Pacific Crest Trail at Cottonwood Pass (11,160 ft / 3,402 m). From there, I would head north past Chicken Spring Lake (11,266 ft / 3,434 m) and enter Sequoia National Park. Crossing Rock Creek and Guyot Pass (10,899 ft / 3,322 m), I would camp at Lower Crabtree Meadow (10,334 ft / 3,150 m) in direct line of sight to Mount Whitney (14,505 ft / 4,421 m), the highest mountain in the contiguous United States. Unfortunately, I did not win a permit for Mount Whitney in the lottery this year, so I would have to come back at some point to summit this mountain gem. Even without Mount Whitney, I needed a permit to camp in Sequoia National Park. Fortunately, Cottonwood Pass no longer has an entry quota past September 15, so securing a non-quota wilderness permit on Recreation.gov was completely straightforward.

Logistically, this trip absolutely needed a shuttle ride. A bike ride was out of the question, both because I didn't want to cycle with a heavy backpack and because the way back would have been more than 50 miles / 80 km with more than 6,500 ft / 1,981 m elevation gain – nothing I would ever be able to do even under the best conditions. I had solicited the help of my friend Chris again, who had previously partnered on my first three-day backpacking trip across the Piute Mountains. He had agreed to give me two shuttle rides every year. This time, it was just him, as his wife Lisa was away at a meditation seminar. We both left the Bay Area in our cars on Friday at noon, stopping briefly on the way in Yosemite National Park to see the smoke cloud of the ongoing Dome Fire from one of the vista points along Tioga Pass Road. Our next stop was Mountain Rambler Brewery in Bishop where we both had a burger for dinner. Their House-Made Veggie Burger was absolutely delicious. The sun had set and we continued south on US-395, then turned right onto Onion Valley Road when we reached the town of Independence. The road up to Kearsarge Pass Trailhead (9,186 ft / 2,800 m) was steep and had plenty of switchbacks. It was a full Harvest Moon and it was shining incredibly brightly on this cloudless night. When we reached the trailhead, it was almost 8pm. There was a surprising number of cars, but I had no issues finding a parking spot to stage my car for the end of the trip. I moved my gear over into Chris's car and we made our way back down the mountain and to Lone Pine where we stayed in the Mount Whitney Motel, marking my third time using it as a Sierra basecamp.

We were back in the car before 6am, driving up Horseshoe Meadow Road to get to the trailhead before sunrise at 6:44am. The familiar switchbacks – rising more than 6,000 ft / 1,829 m from the desert floor of Owens Valley – provided stunning views up and down the valley. Chris and I both watched the thermometer in the car as it was slowly dropping degree by degree. When we reached the trailhead at 9,936 ft / 3,028 m, it was a crisp 32°F / 0°C. There were still plenty of cars at the trailhead though. Chris was only wearing shorts, as he was off to Sonora afterwards to play golf, so I hurried to put on my gear and get ready. We took a selfie at the trailhead sign and I set out at 6:41am. I had not put on that many layers, so my brisk walk was also to warm me up from the inside. I quickly reached the junction with Trail Pass Trail where I had turned left every time on my previous two trips toward Trail Pass (10,469 ft / 3,191 m) and Mulkey Pass. Now I was heading straight to stay on Cottonwood Pass Trail for the very first time. The trail stayed within the lodgepole and foxtail pines on the northern side of Horseshoe Meadow, where a heavy frost blanketed the open grassland and the morning alpenglow ignited the granite summit of Trail Peak (11,601 ft / 3,536 m) to the south.

Then it was time for seemingly endless switchbacks to get me to Cottonwood Pass. As the trail began climbing the western headwall above Horseshoe Meadow, ancient foxtail pines (*Pinus balfouriana*) with thick, deeply furrowed cinnamon bark and weathered amber snags flanked the sandy tread. Below, the ascending trail cut through golden shrubby willow slopes, with the rugged granitic ramparts of the Sierra Crest rising in the distance under clear morning skies.

The sun had crested the mountains to the east and was illuminating Horseshoe Meadow now. Gazing back east from the higher switchbacks at 10,707 ft / 3,263 m, the meandering stream channels and lodgepole pine woodlands of the basin spread out in full daylight, framed against the distant, arid wall of the Inyo Mountains rising beyond the hazy desert trench of the Owens Valley. I was grateful that there was no wind because the air was still very cold.

[PHOTO: 281A5931.jpg][CAPTION: Gazing east across the expansive Horseshoe Meadow basin from the Cottonwood Pass switchbacks, morning sunlight illuminates the winding creek channels and lodgepole pine forests at 10,707 ft / 3,263 m. On the far horizon, the rugged, arid spine of the Inyo Mountains rises dramatically beyond the hazy desert trench of the Owens Valley. (36°26'42.22" N 118°12'35.08" W)]

Back on Cottonwood Pass (11,160 ft / 3,402 m), I was reunited with the Pacific Crest Trail, but now I headed north. Just north of the saddle, the trail offered a superb parting panorama looking east-southeast across Horseshoe Meadow toward the rounded granite dome of Peak 11,062 ft / 3,372 m and the deep notch where Cottonwood Canyon cuts down to the Owens Valley.

[PHOTO: 281A5933.jpg][CAPTION: Looking east-southeast across Horseshoe Meadow from the Pacific Crest Trail just north of Cottonwood Pass, morning sunlight illuminates the forested basin floor and the prominent rounded granitic summit of Peak 11,062 ft / 3,372 m. In the far distance, the deep cleft of Cottonwood Canyon drops toward the haze of Owens Valley beneath the rugged spine of the Inyo Mountains. (36°27'0.71" N 118°13'0.69" W)]

The trail continued to gain more elevation through weathered foxtail pines (*Pinus balfouriana*), a remarkable high-elevation conifer with one of the most curious botanical distributions in North America. The southern Sierra subspecies (*Pinus balfouriana* subsp. *austrina*) is separated from its northern counterpart in the Klamath Mountains by an extraordinary 300-mile / 480-km gap without a single tree in between. Thriving at the subalpine fringe and living upwards of 2,000 to 2,500 years, this stretch of trail traverses the prime heart of its range before the species abruptly reaches its northern Sierra terminus near the Kings-Kern Divide.

A little over half a mile later, I took a spur trail north that led me to Chicken Spring Lake (11,266 ft / 3,434 m). At PCT Mile 750.8, this was the very first natural lake along the entire Pacific Crest Trail northbound from Mexico, as all water bodies south of here were man-made reservoirs. Surrounded by glaciated granite slopes on all but one side, it was a beautiful alpine tarn nestled beneath Cirque Peak (12,900 ft / 3,932 m). Cirque Peak marks the southernmost limit of Pleistocene cirque glaciation along the Sierra Crest; south of Cottonwood Pass, the mountains were never heavily glaciated during the ice ages, which is why Horseshoe Meadow below remained a gentle, rolling upland basin rather than a carved glacial cirque. Sunlight illuminated the turquoise shallows and clean granodiorite shoreline as gentle ripples reflected the towering cirque headwall.

[PHOTO: 281A5940.jpg][CAPTION: Sunlight illuminates the turquoise shallows and granodiorite shoreline of Chicken Spring Lake (11,266 ft / 3,434 m), the first natural glacial lake along the Pacific Crest Trail northbound from Mexico. Across the pristine waters, the steep talus slopes of the cirque headwall rise toward the Sierra Crest beneath a brilliant autumn sky. (36°27'8.37" N 118°13'40.54" W)]

A few backpackers were just breaking camp and another one was sitting at the lake shore filtering water. I also needed to filter water. I had not brought up much in anticipation of the lake and in order to save weight on the switchbacks. The water was cold, and there was a thin layer of ice on the very shallow side of the lake. Submerged granodiorite boulders rested in the clear water beneath a prominent white bathtub waterline ring along the rocky shore marking early-season high water, while foxtail pine krummholz clung stubbornly to the talus slopes ~1,100 ft / 335 m above. I didn't stay too long, as there was a mild breeze and I didn't want to cool off too much.

[PHOTO: 281A5944.jpg][CAPTION: Sunlight shimmers across the clear, pale green shallows of Chicken Spring Lake, revealing submerged granodiorite boulders and the distinctive white bathtub ring along the rocky shoreline marking early-season high water. Weathered foxtail pine krummholz clings to the talus slopes of the cirque headwall rising ~1,100 ft / 335 m above the alpine tarn. (36°27'12.18" N 118°13'43.08" W)]

Leaving the lakeshore along the outlet trail, a remarkable discovery caught my eye in the damp, frost-thawed granitic sand: a fresh, perfectly formed direct-register paw print of an American black bear (*Ursus americanus*). The bear had been foraging during its autumn hyperphagia, stepping its hind foot cleanly into its front track and pressing five distinct, rounded toe pads and overlapping palm impressions into the trail tread.

[PHOTO: 281A5945.jpg][CAPTION: A fresh direct-register track of an American black bear (*Ursus americanus*) pressed into the damp, frosty decomposed granite trail tread at Chicken Spring Lake outlet. The hind foot has stepped directly into the front print, creating a characteristic overlapping impression with five distinct rounded toe pads. (36°27'13.79" N 118°13'45.42" W)]

The trail wound past the outlet through a golden late-season sedge meadow, where a young, vigorous foxtail pine stood as a hardy pioneer conifer beneath the glaciated granite headwall of Cirque Peak rising ~1,000 ft / 305 m above into the azure Sierra sky.

[PHOTO: 281A5949.jpg][CAPTION: A young, vigorous foxtail pine (*Pinus balfouriana*) stands as a pioneer conifer in the golden late-season sedge meadow flanking the outlet stream of Chicken Spring Lake at 11,278 ft / 3,438 m. Behind it, the glaciated granite headwall of the Cirque Peak ridge rises dramatically ~1,000 ft / 305 m into the azure Sierra sky. (36°27'14.39" N 118°13'47.16" W)]

Beyond the lake, it was another long switchback, but at the end of it was a rocky outcrop that offered spectacular views towards Big Whitney Meadow where I had bushwhacked during the last trip, and beyond to the jagged crest of the Great Western Divide. Rooted into granodiorite grus at 11,466 ft / 3,495 m, weathered foxtail pines framed the vast subalpine basin below, while the pale talus summit of Kern Peak (11,510 ft / 3,508 m) anchored the southern skyline. Two young guys were standing there as well, one of them shirtless and in shorts which made me shiver a bit. They had camped at the lake and were going to summit Cirque Peak today. They both seemed very energetic. I soaked in the view a bit more before I continued. It was a bit more uphill to the day's high point along the ridge at 11,507 ft / 3,507 m, but then the trail stayed roughly at the same elevation.

[PHOTO: 281A5954.jpg][CAPTION: Looking southwest from a rocky outcrop along the Pacific Crest Trail above Chicken Spring Lake, the expansive subalpine flats of Big Whitney Meadow unfold across the basin below. On the distant horizon, the pale talus summit of Kern Peak anchors the left skyline, while the jagged granitic crest of the Great Western Divide rises dramatically to the west beyond Johnson Peak. Weathered foxtail pines frame the foreground, rooted into granodiorite grus at 11,466 ft / 3,495 m along the Sierra Crest. (36°27'18.45" N 118°13'46.00" W)]

Through the trees, I started seeing Siberian Pass and the vast sandy flat of Siberian Outpost beyond, stretching toward the massive rounded dome of Mount Guyot (12,300 ft / 3,749 m) and the towering talus slopes of Joe Devel Peak (13,327 ft / 4,062 m). In the far distance across the Kern River trench, the rugged crest of Kern Point (12,789 ft / 3,898 m) and the jagged ramparts of the Great Western Divide broke the northwest horizon. The wide expanse of decomposed granite sand earned its name in the nineteenth century for its resemblance to bleak Siberian tundra. Geologically, plateaus like Siberian Outpost and Guyot Flat are unglaciated remnants of an ancient, pre-Pleistocene erosion surface. While massive ice age glaciers gouged out the deep, cliff-walled trenches of Rock Creek and the Kern River Canyon, these high, gentle uplands remained largely ice-free. Without glacial scouring to strip the rock down to polished bedrock, millennia of severe freeze-thaw cycles – frost wedging – slowly pulverized the granodiorite into deep beds of coarse sand known as grus.

[PHOTO: 281A5985.jpg][CAPTION: A weathered foxtail pine frames an expansive panoramic vista across the sandy flats of Siberian Outpost toward the rounded summit dome of Mount Guyot and the towering talus slopes of Joe Devel Peak. Beyond the Rock Creek drainage, the distant peaks of Kern Point and the Great Western Divide crest along the northwest horizon beneath a brilliant autumn sky. (36°28'19.05" N 118°15'26.58" W)]

The PCT rounded another basin, offering one last look at Big Whitney Meadow nestled ~1,600 ft / 488 m below in the Golden Trout Wilderness, with Kern Peak anchoring the undulating crest of the Toowa Range across the rolling Kern Plateau.

[PHOTO: 281A5989.jpg][CAPTION: Weathered foxtail pines frame an expansive southward vista overlooking the golden expanse of Big Whitney Meadow nestled ~1,600 ft / 488 m below in the Golden Trout Wilderness. On the distant horizon, the isolated pyramidal summit of Kern Peak anchors the undulating crest of the Toowa Range across the Kern Plateau. Wispy cirrus clouds drift overhead under a brilliant late-morning High Sierra sky. (36°28'23.83" N 118°15'33.65" W)]

A moment later, the trail reached a weather-beaten wooden boundary sign marking the threshold into Sequoia National Park at 11,320 ft / 3,450 m, which was established back in 1890 as California's first national park. From here, it was more than 7 miles / 11 km downhill to Rock Creek.

[PHOTO: 281A5991.jpg][CAPTION: A weather-beaten National Park Service boundary sign marks the southern entrance to Sequoia National Park along the Pacific Crest Trail at 11,320 ft / 3,450 m. Weathered foxtail pines frame the sandy trail as it descends toward the Rock Creek drainage, with the rounded granite summit of Joe Devel Peak (13,327 ft / 4,062 m) visible in the northwest distance. (36°28'25.79" N 118°15'36.70" W)]

I took another break on a rock on the side of the trail and while I was resting, a horse caravan passed; they were pack stock being led into the backcountry, a traditional Sierra operation out of local pack stations bringing supplies further up north. Shortly thereafter, I crossed Upper Rock Creek Trail. It was pleasantly gentle downhill for a while across the northern margin of the Siberian Outpost plateau, where the bleached decomposed granite sands and scattered boulders opened up sweeping views of Joe Devel Peak's unglaciated southern talus slopes and the distant Mount Chamberlin ridge.

[PHOTO: 281A5999.jpg][CAPTION: Looking across the bleached granite sands of the Siberian Outpost plateau, the massive talus-covered southern dome of Joe Devel Peak (13,327 ft / 4,062 m) rises ~2.4 mi / 3.8 km to the north above the Rock Creek drainage. Weathered Sierra foxtail pines frame the sunlit basin, their distinctive bottle-brush branches thriving in the harsh, high-elevation alpine terrain. Scattered granite boulders and young conifer saplings dot the sandy flat beneath a brilliant Sierra sky. (36°28'51.63" N 118°18'2.48" W)]

To the south across the canyon, I could see the high granitic massif of Mount Anna Mills (12,054 ft / 3,674 m), while further north, the massive dome of Mount Guyot (12,300 ft / 3,749 m) dominated the horizon above the deep Rock Creek drainage. The mountain was named in 1881 in honor of Swiss-American geologist Arnold Guyot, who studied glacial dynamics alongside Louis Agassiz. Guyot's surname gained global fame in oceanography as well, where flat-topped undersea volcanoes are officially called "guyots." One readily obvious difference compared to hiking outside of the national park was that trail junctions were more nicely marked with proper guideposts and distances. I passed the junction with Rock Creek Lake Trail and now headed west across a sunlit, golden subalpine meadow basin where a single-track stretch of the PCT wound through cured sedges and lodgepole pines toward Mount Guyot.

[PHOTO: 281A6011.jpg][CAPTION: A single-track stretch of the Pacific Crest Trail winds through a sunlit, golden subalpine meadow in the upper Rock Creek drainage. In the distance, the massive granodiorite dome of Mount Guyot rises above a bordering forest of lodgepole pines under a wispy autumn sky. (36°29'39.59" N 118°19'38.63" W)]

I could hear Rock Creek close by. Then the five horses from earlier turned back onto the trail. Apparently, they had dropped gear at the Rock Creek Ranger Station, home to a historic 1930s log patrol cabin. Knowing that I still had a few miles ahead of me, I didn't take any detours though and followed the horses. They were leaving a noticeable dust cloud behind.

Half a mile later, I reached the Rock Creek crossing at 9,519 ft / 2,901 m, the lowest point of the entire day. The horses were right ahead of me and one of them had stopped in the middle of the creek and was peeing into the water. That's why you always collect water upstream of any crossing. The creek, which drains the high crest around Mount Langley toward the Kern River, was about 20 feet / 6 m wide, but it was fairly straightforward to hop over rocks and cross without getting your feet wet. I stopped on the other side. I had another 6 or so miles / 10 km ahead of me before camp, so I needed to filter some more water. While I was waiting, I marveled at the stream, watching the crystal-clear riffles glint across amber pebbles and mossy boulders beneath towering lodgepole pines and golden autumn willows, while enjoying the warm sun rays.

[PHOTO: 281A6019.jpg][CAPTION: Sunlight glints across the amber pebbles and crystal-clear riffles of Rock Creek at the Pacific Crest Trail crossing. Lined by towering Sierra lodgepole pines and golden autumn willows, the rock-strewn stream provides a tranquil rest stop at the day's lowest elevation point before the steep ascent toward Guyot Pass. (36°29'47.11" N 118°20'15.28" W)]

But then it was time to get going again. On the other side of Rock Creek, it was very steeply uphill. The trail gained almost 1,000 ft / 305 m in less than a mile. The switchbacks were relentless and I had to take a lot of small breaks. This was exhausting! Climbing out of the canyon, I was rewarded with an expansive look-back vista across Rock Creek canyon to Mount Anna Mills (12,054 ft / 3,674 m), its broad summit dome and steep north-facing cirque headwalls rising majestically above the timbered canyon, while a patch of subalpine willows glowed in vibrant autumn russet in a drainage ravine across the valley.

[PHOTO: 281A6021.jpg][CAPTION: Mount Anna Mills (12,054 ft / 3,674 m) rises majestically across Rock Creek canyon, framed by Sierra lodgepole pines on the steep switchbacks climbing toward Guyot Pass. Across the drainage, a patch of subalpine willows glows with autumn russet beneath the granite summit. (36°29'54.16" N 118°20'31.86" W)]

The gradient eased for a while as the trail entered an open foxtail pine woodland on the southern approach to Guyot Pass. Fresh droppings along the decomposed granite tread marked the recent passage of the pack horses ahead, while a striking weather-sculpted dead snag glowed amber in the late afternoon sun against the rising forested slopes.

[PHOTO: 281A6025.jpg][CAPTION: The Pacific Crest Trail climbs gently through open foxtail pine woodland on the southern approach toward Guyot Pass, flanked by living conifers and a weather-beaten, amber-hued dead snag. Fresh horse droppings along the decomposed granite tread reveal the recent passage of backcountry pack stock heading north into Sequoia National Park. Afternoon sunlight filters through the subalpine forest, illuminating the rising forested slopes that lead up to the pass. (36°30'39.71" N 118°20'44.12" W)]

I crossed Guyot Creek at ~10,500 ft / 3,200 m, but then it was steeply uphill one last time to tackle Guyot Pass (10,899 ft / 3,322 m). The sun was lower on the horizon now and clouds in the distance were dimming its rays. Guyot Pass didn't offer any particularly stunning views, but it was a good spot to take one last break before the final push.

It was clear that I wouldn't make it to camp with much daylight left, so I tried to pick up the pace coming down the pass. The trail rounded Guyot Flat on the left, an arid subalpine expanse of bleached decomposed granite sand framed by mature, cinnamon-barked foxtail pines, beneath the imposing granitic pyramid of Mount Guyot rising ~1,636 ft / 499 m above the basin.

[PHOTO: 281A6033.jpg][CAPTION: Framed by mature foxtail pines, the massive granitic pyramid of Mount Guyot rises across the arid decomposed granite expanse of Guyot Flat. Late afternoon sunlight illuminates the open subalpine basin as the Pacific Crest Trail skirts the sandy plateau toward Crabtree Meadow. (36°31'30.02" N 118°21'3.90" W)]

The trail then angled north again. Unfortunately, there was still an uphill stretch that I had not expected, just south of Crabtree Meadow. Finally over the last ridge, the trail switchbacked down across large granite boulders. Crabtree Meadow stretched out in the distance below the towering western ramparts of Mount Whitney (14,505 ft / 4,421 m) and the dramatic vertical pinnacles of Keeler Needle (14,265 ft / 4,348 m) and Crooks Peak (14,000 ft / 4,267 m), flanked by Mount Young (13,176 ft / 4,016 m) and Mount Hitchcock (13,186 ft / 4,019 m) under golden late-afternoon light. The neighboring peaks carry deep scientific and mountaineering history: Keeler Needle was named for James Edward Keeler, a 23-year-old assistant on Samuel Langley's 1881 solar expedition who later proved Saturn's rings rotate differentially, while Mount Young and Mount Hitchcock were named that same year for Princeton astronomer Charles A. Young and geologist Edward Hitchcock. Just to the south, Crooks Peak – formerly Day Needle – was renamed by Congress in 1990 to honor Hulda Crooks, affectionately known as "Grandma Whitney," who scaled Mount Whitney 23 times between the ages of 66 and 91.

[PHOTO: 281A6041.jpg][CAPTION: Late afternoon light illuminates the vast granite amphitheater of the High Sierra as the Pacific Crest Trail switchbacks down toward the golden grasses of Lower Crabtree Meadow. Across the basin, the massive western ramparts of Mount Whitney and the sharp spires of Keeler Needle and Crooks Peak rise majestically between Mount Young and the long shoulder of Mount Hitchcock. Weathered foxtail pines and glaciated granodiorite boulders anchor the rocky descent into camp. (36°32'53.47" N 118°21'37.42" W)]

On the descent, a yellow-bellied marmot (*Marmota flaviventris*) paused atop a sun-warmed boulder to watch me pass before ducking into the talus, undoubtedly fattening up before entering winter hibernation. I had to round one more rocky outcrop, but then I was finally at the campsite I had targeted in Lower Crabtree Meadow along Crabtree Creek. The meadow bears the name of pioneer brothers William "Bill" and Fletcher Crabtree, who pastured cattle and horses in these high subalpine flats during the 1870s and 1880s before the creation of Sequoia National Park. Golden late-afternoon light illuminated the broad granitic crest of Mount Hitchcock as the clear creek meandered peacefully through the autumn sedges, with the distant pinnacles of the Whitney Crest gleaming above the lodgepole pine forest.

[PHOTO: 281A6046.jpg][CAPTION: Golden late-afternoon sunlight illuminates the broad granitic crest of Mount Hitchcock as Crabtree Creek meanders through the autumn sedges of Lower Crabtree Meadow. In the distance to the left, the jagged granite pinnacles of the Whitney Crest rise above the shadowed lodgepole pine forest. This tranquil setting greeted camp at the conclusion of a 21.2 mi / 34.1 km trek from Cottonwood Pass Trailhead. (36°33'6.83" N 118°21'29.23" W)]

After more than 21 miles / 34 km, this had been a long day and I could very much feel it. With the sun gone, it was starting to cool down quickly. I grabbed water from nearby Crabtree Creek and filtered it. Dinner was Stowaway Gourmet Chana Masala mixed with some plant-based jerky – hearty and delicious! I still had some leftover baguette from the morning to round it out. After putting my bear canister into the nearby bear box, I retreated to my tent. It was almost dark now anyway. I ended up wearing two layers of pants and three layers around my torso to keep me warm. I fell asleep early, sometime after 8pm, utterly exhausted from the day.

----------
Trail Conditions: All trails were in good condition and easy to follow. Both Cottonwood Pass Trail and the Pacific Crest Trail were quite sandy at times.

Parking: There is plenty of room for cars at the trailhead.

Crowds: Lightly traveled. Only met 20 backpackers throughout the day as well as 5 horses with their handler. When I camped, a couple had set up their tent about 100 yards / 91 m away from me. Other than that, I was by myself.

Wildlife: There were plenty of chipmunks and ground squirrels throughout the day as well as a yellow-bellied marmot (*Marmota flaviventris*) on the descent towards Lower Crabtree Meadow, and a fresh American black bear (*Ursus americanus*) direct-register paw print in the damp sand at Chicken Spring Lake outlet.

Weather: Mostly sunny with some scattered clouds throughout the entire day. Slightly increased cloud coverage towards the evening. Low of 32°F / 0°C at the beginning of the hike. High of 56°F / 13°C along Rock Creek in the early afternoon.

Land Manager & Permits: Golden Trout Wilderness. Inyo National Forest. Sequoia National Park. Wilderness permit required (no quota).

Overall Rating: 3.9
