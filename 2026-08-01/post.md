# Olancha Pass Trail to Trail Pass (Day 1)

Aug 1, 2026 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Central California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/central-california/)

**Start:** [36.448336, -118.170638](https://maps.app.goo.gl/DmW1mNZnJkUwNtfk9) (Cottonwood Pass Trailhead)  
**End:** [36.275525, -118.168028](https://maps.app.goo.gl/XE7tv2cJCfpdCY8h7) (Brown Cow Camp)

**Distance:** 17.8 mi (up to PCT mile 721.8, 27.2% completed)  
**Time:** 10 hours 2 minutes (start 6:04am, end 4:15pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 1,759 ft (low 8,376 ft – high 10,469 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 2,953 ft (start 9,936 ft – end 8,742 ft)

---

I was back for another stretch of the Pacific Crest Trail. California was suffering from yet another heat wave. The temperatures in Lone Pine – the gateway town to Mount Whitney within Owens Valley – were forecast at 107°F / 42°C. Hiking within the Sierra Nevada, more than 5,000 feet higher, I was hoping for more manageable temperatures and merciful mosquitoes. This stretch was going to be a 3 day backpacking trip, mostly through Golden Trout Wilderness in Inyo National Forest. Starting from the high-altitude rim near Horseshoe Meadow, the first day of this Kern Plateau loop would drop me off the granite Sierra crest and plunge into the expansive, silent meadow basins of the South Fork Kern River watershed. Traversing over 17 miles across loose granite sands, pine forests, and wide-open golden meadows, this opening stretch would offer a remote, sub-alpine backcountry experience at the heart of Golden Trout Wilderness with constant views of surrounding peaks like Trail Peak, Templeton Mountain, and Olancha Peak. I would cross the South Fork Kern River twice and then camp somewhere near the boundary with the South Sierra Wilderness. Only the second day would get me back onto the PCT via Olancha Pass Trail where I had left off the last time.

![The Trail Pass junction with the PCT (36°25’37.8137″ N 118°10’34.6772″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4427-1024x683.jpg)

With the prospect of two nights in a tent, I opted for a motel for the night before the hike. Lone Pine was about 40 minutes away from the trailhead. Coincidentally, it was also the halfway point on the eastern side of the Sierra Nevada. Going south via Tehachapi and Bakersfield or north via Yosemite National Park takes roughly the same time even though the southern route is about 100 miles longer. The drive through Owens Valley was scenic as usual and I arrived in Lone Pine at 8:40pm. I went to bed early to get enough rest for a 5am start the next morning. The first few miles out of Lone Pine were along Whitney Portal Road which I would eventually take to get to Mount Whitney, the highest point in the contiguous United States, with an elevation of 14,505 feet / 4,421 meters. But that would be the story of another day. I soon turned south onto Horseshoe Meadow Road which switchbacked beautifully up the eastern Sierra Nevada escarpment as the sun was coming up.

![Taking a break with first glimpses of Mulkey Meadows (36°24’50.508″ N 118°11’23.946″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4432-1024x683.jpg)

![Entering Mulkey Meadows (36°24’30.894″ N 118°11’37.2″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4438-1024x683.jpg)

The Cottonwood Pass Trailhead parking lot was enormous, built to accommodate hundreds of cars – a testament to how popular this high-country corridor is. I had no trouble finding a parking spot at this early hour, in fact I was able to park right opposite the trail. A few cars over, two people had slept in their car and were just getting up. The sun was up but not reaching the trailhead just yet. It was crisp at 54°F / 12°C but I knew that would soon change. I packed the last few items and double checked that I had everything that I needed, then set out west, shortly after 6am. After a quarter-mile on Cottonwood Pass Trail, I branched left onto Trail Pass Trail. Leaving the pine canopy behind, the trail opened into Horseshow Meadow, crossing a small tributary to Cottonwood Creek. In the distance, the pale, bare granitic dome of Cirque Peak loomed beneath a cloudless blue sky as the warm early morning sun began illuminating the sandy flats.

![A Sierra Gentian in Mulkey Meadows (36°24’1.2906″ N 118°12’30.3656″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4457-1024x683.jpg)

![Looking back at Trail Peak from Mulkey Meadows (36°23’57.72″ N 118°12’30.852″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4462-1024x683.jpg)

![Ramshaw Meadows with Kern Peak in the distance (36°21’39.51″ N 118°13’39.5288″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4479-1024x683.jpg)

Turning south, the trail began a moderate, sustained climb towards Trail Pass. Glancing left through the timber, I caught glimpses of Round Valley – one of Horseshoe Meadow’s long, grassy arms. Near the final switchbacks up to the pass, I met a day-hiking couple with their dog. Reaching the crest at Trail Pass (10,469 ft) brought me to a prominent junction where the trail crosses the Pacific Crest Trail. Marked by a weathered wooden signpost pointing towards Cottonwood Pass and Mulkey Pass, the pass sits tucked beneath high-altitude lodgepole and foxtail pines. The views from the pass were mostly obscured by trees, but I could see glimpses of Sharknose Ridge to the south and Mulkey Meadows in between. The High Sierra was truly unfolding in front of me. From the pass, I began a 2 mile descent across loose, sandy switchbacks. As the trail dropped out of heavy timber, clear openings through the pines offered first golden glimpses of Mulkey Meadows resting peacefully in the basin below.

I paused on the sandy slope to take a quick break and enjoy the view. Mulkey Meadows was neatly nestled among the mountains but not as lush and green as I had expected. Reaching the basin floor, the sandy singletrack opened up into the expansive floor of Mulkey Meadows. Surrounded by low, pine-forested ridges, the wide valley felt completely untouched and quiet. Continuing south, the trail followed Mulkey Creek upstream across the open basin. Looking back towards the north, Trail Peak stood impressively behind the sweeping, sun-drenched grassy flats. The trail crossed a few smaller side creeks along the way. One of them looked deceptively dry at first, but stepping into the tall grass, I unexpectedly sank knee-deep into murky wetland mud. This was not what I had planned for. Without time to swap into water shoes, I pulled myself out and kept hiking, letting my boots dry on the move.

![Hiking across Templeton Meadows with Kern Peak in the distance (36°20’5.8927″ N 118°13’32.4889″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4502-1024x683.jpg)

Gaining elevation once more, the path crossed a high saddle between McConnel Meadow and Overholster Meadow before plunging rapidly toward Ramshaw Meadows. By late morning, the heat wave was making itself felt even at 9,000 feet. The trail opened up into Ramshaw Meadows, a long, drawn-out basin framed by low pine ridges. Across the expansive meadow flats, the pale, granite peak of Kern Peak rose prominently on the horizon, guiding my way south. Traversing the dry, sandy saddles between the meadow basins, I spotted clusters of Woolly Sunflower blooming right out of the arid, gravelly dirt – its silver-grey felted leaves and bright yellow petals engineered perfectly for the intense high-altitude midday sun.

After crossing Ramshaw Meadows, I climbed another saddle that led me directly towards Templeton Meadows. Emerging from the trees onto the sandy upper edge of the meadow, the distinctive, heavily timbered dome of Templeton Mountain (9,975 ft) rose directly ahead. In the distance to the left, the rugged, massive bulk of Olancha Peak loomed on the horizon – a striking reminder of where I would be on the second day. Unlike the dramatic granite spires of the High Sierra crest, Templeton Mountain is an extinct volcanic cone. It features a broad, rounded structure that rises over 1,200 feet above the surrounding plateau and meadow basin. The area was wide open now. Spotting a solitary pine offering deep shade on the edge of the meadow, I dropped my backpack against the trunk and slouched into the dirt. A cool, gentle breeze swept across the valley as I took in the sweeping views, even dozing off for a few minutes.

![Entering Fat Cow Meadow with Olancha Peak prominently ahead (36°18’8.7962″ N 118°12’30.8805″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4559-1024x683.jpg)

Eventually, it was time to shoulder the backpack again and press forward. The dirt singletrack cut a clear path through the wide, sage-dotted flats of Templeton Meadows, with the granitic summit of Kern Peak framing the horizon to the southwest. Ahead of me was the first crossing of the South Fork Kern River. Near its headwaters, the river was intimate and inviting – only about five feet wide and barely a foot deep, winding gently through golden meadow grasses and lush willow thickets with Templeton Mountain rising in the background. Since my boots were already wet from the earlier marsh crossing in Mulkey Meadows, I stepped straight into the crystal-clear current without hesitation. On the far side of the river, the faint trail tread faded into the tall meadow turf. Pausing to navigate across the wide basin, I took in a breathtaking panoramic view looking back north across Templeton Meadows. In the far distance, the pale, granite peaks of the High Sierra crest – including the prominent dome of Cirque Peak—stretched dramatically across the horizon beneath a cloudless summer sky.

Rejoining a clearer path coming from Ramshaw Meadows, I turned east along the base of Templeton Mountain, crossing over a low saddle just south of the peak and heading along dry Strawberry Creek towards Fat Cow Meadow. As I entered the meadow, the trail tread was still faint but visible in the dirt. Directly ahead on the eastern horizon, the impressive bulk of Olancha Peak dominated the view. Initially still somewhat visible, the trail soon fizzled out entirely across Fat Cow Meadow, requiring me to consult my offline map to maintain the right general direction while picking my way through the tall grass and sagebrush. The path finally reappeared as I drew closer to Strawberry Meadows. Here, the landscape opened up into a broad, peaceful basin surrounded by low, granitic hills and pine-covered ridges stretching out toward the horizon.

It was close to 3pm in the afternoon heat when I reached the second crossing of the South Fork Kern River. Tucked against a rugged rock outcrop, the river had widened into a tranquil, crystal-clear pool, reflecting the surrounding pines and golden granite boulders in the afternoon light. It was already much wider here after picking up numerous creeks along the way. Stepping across the shallow, gravelly ford, I turned back to look at the river gently flowing past massive granite boulders and fallen pine timber. It was easily one of the most picturesque and serene rest spots of the day, so I took another break on the eastern side of the river. I filtered 4 liters / 1 gallon of water, half of it to fill my water bottles, half of it to gulp down on the spot. I had settled on a campsite for the night at Brown Cow Camp, about 1.8 miles further. It looked like there would be another creek nearby, so I wouldn’t have to ration my water, but I planned for dry camping just in case. It was bittersweet leaving my shaded break spot, but I had to keep going. It was uphill again and the afternoon heat did not make it easy. Temperatures had reached 97°F / 36°C by now. Then I finally reached Brown Cow Camp, a historic backcountry cattle camp near Brown Meadow. Lush and wide, the meadow grass was dotted with late-summer white wildflowers, sweeping gently towardx dense lodgepole pine borders. In the far distance, the subtle ridges of the High Sierra crest provided a classic backcountry backdrop.

![A Fritillary Butterfly on a Mountain Aster (36°16’32.52″ N 118°10’2.064″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4580-1024x683.jpg)

Tucked among Jeffrey and lodgepole pines, this camp originated as a summertime headquarters for cattle ranchers. Ranchers used these outposts to manage herds grazing the high-altitude meadows. While commercial grazing in the wilderness has been significantly scaled back over the decades, these cow camps remain historic relics of the region’s ranching heritage. The camp still featured multiple shattered buildings with historic wooden fences and a great view across Brown Meadow. I sat down my backpack and looked for water. But unfortunately, the nearby creek was dry. So I had to make due with the water that I had brought. I set up my tent, then started making food. I had two courses today: [Good-to-Go Indian Vegetable Korma](https://www.garagegrowngear.com/products/indian-vegetable-korma-by-good-to-go) followed by [Good-to-Go Mushroom Risotto](https://www.garagegrowngear.com/products/herbed-mushroom-risotto-by-good-to-go). It was delicious. The sun was slowly setting and the trees among the camp were casting longer shadows. More mosquitoes were out and about so I retreated to my tent at 7:30pm for an early bedtime. It was still hot and I didn’t need my sleeping bag at all. Sleep came quickly and was much needed.

---

**Trail Conditions:** Trail Pass Trail is quite sandy on both sides of Trail Pass but on firmer ground once you get closer to Mulkey Meadows. Within Mulkey Meadows and across Templeton Meadows after crossing the South Fork Kern River, the trail is only faintly visible a few times but you usually find it again quickly. Within Fat Cow Meadow and parts of Strawberry Meadow, the trail disappears entirely and a good offline map is strongly recommended. Past the second crossing of the South Fork Kern River, the trail is easy to follow.

**Parking:** There is plenty of room for cars at the trailhead.

**Crowds:** Very lightly traveled. Only met 2 day hikers near the trailhead and 2 backpackers near Mulkey Meadows.

**Weather:** Sunny with no clouds throughout the entire day. A light intermittent breeze in the afternoon. Low of 54°F / 12°C in the morning at the beginning of the hike. High of 97°F / 36°C near the second crossing of the South Fork Kern River. Humidity between 28% in the morning and 13% in the afternoon.

**Land Manager & Permits:** Golden Trout Wilderness. Inyo National Forest. Wilderness permit required (no quota).

**Overall Rating:** 3.8

Difficulty: Strenuous  
Scenery: Great  
Logistics: Very Easy
