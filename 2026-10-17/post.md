After getting caught in the first snowstorm of the season at the end of my last trip, I had been watching the weather forecast carefully. A week later it was sunny with warm temperatures. This late in the season, I expected that it would be easy to get a permit, especially when the daily quota was 60. However, all permits were gone when I tried to get one for Saturday, October 10. Sunday still had permits, so I figured I would have to hike on Sunday and Monday. For the shuttle ride from my car to the trailhead, I was lucky to get local trail angel and legend Kurt Power aka Lone Pine Kurt to give me a ride on Sunday morning at 8am. When I checked the weather forecast again a day later, it had radically changed to rain and snow showers. And that was still the unfortunate reality on Friday right before the trip. I canceled my ride with Kurt and had almost given up on another PCT trip this year when the following weekend looked even better with clear skies and highs in the 60s°F / ~16 – 21°C. This time I was able to get a permit for Saturday. However, Kurt was out of town and referred me to Mark from Independence. Mark was still available on Saturday morning and agreed to give me a ride.

This trip would take me from Onion Valley, west of Independence, back up to Kearsarge Pass and down the Bullfrog Lake Trail cutoff to rejoin the PCT where I had left off. From there, I would head north across Glen Pass and camp at the Rae Lakes, a particularly beautiful set of lakes within Kings Canyon National Park. On the second day, I would continue north on the PCT for a bit before taking Baxter Pass Trail east past Baxter Lakes and across Baxter Pass down to the Baxter Pass Trailhead at the bottom of the Eastern Sierra. When I arrived at the Baxter Pass Trailhead on Friday evening, shortly after 8pm, it was already dark. The road up to the trailhead was unpaved but in very good condition. The trailhead itself was small and not very flat, so it took me a few minutes to position the car in a way that my rooftop tent was reasonably level. When I finally turned off the engine and the lights, I could see the stars and the Milky Way above me. It was beautiful! I settled into the tent, had dinner, and crawled into my sleeping bag. It was not too cold and a hoodie was enough to keep me warm.

When my alarm went off at 6:45am, the sun wasn't up yet, but it was light enough to see without my headlamp. There was absolutely no wind and it was completely quiet – a very peaceful experience. I packed my backpack and had a bite to eat. After the sun came up, it brightly illuminated North Fork Oak Creek Canyon where I would come down the following day, catching the high granite ridges of Mount Mary Austin and Mount Baxter on either side of the reddish-brown Sierra Crest headwall near Baxter Pass.

Mark arrived shortly before 8am as planned. He was an older gentleman, well into retirement, and frequently gave hikers shuttle rides. We chatted on the way back to Independence and up Onion Valley. Mark had been a fisherman in Stinson Beach in Marin County, just north of San Francisco, for over 40 years before he moved to Independence where his brother lived. He showed me a photo of his boat from back then. As we drove across North Fork Oak Creek, Mark vividly told me about the afternoon monsoon on July 12, 2008 that turned the creek into a roaring mud and debris flow, obliterating everything in its path and destroying Oak Creek Campground and most of the Mt. Whitney Fish Hatchery. A camper named Don Rockwood was inside his motorhome at the North Fork campground when the surge hit. The torrent ripped his RV apart, and he was thrown into the flow and survived by riding the top of the moving mud and logs for roughly half a mile / 0.8 km before scrambling onto high ground.

As we drove past the Mt. Whitney Fish Hatchery, Mark pointed out the historic 1917 stone buildings with their massive rock walls that had withstood the mudflow. The flooding had ended all active fish-rearing operations and turned the area into a public picnic site. When the hatchery first opened in 1917, its primary mission was propagating the California golden trout, and it relied on a remote backcountry egg-taking station established at Rae Lakes – my destination for tonight. Between 1917 and 1927, hardy mule packers collected millions of wild trout eggs in the high basin and hauled them in aerated milk cans strapped to pack mules across these very alpine passes down to the hatchery raceways in Independence. Finally on Onion Valley Road, we passed the Eastern California Museum and Mark shared more about the railroad history of the area, a story of ambitious silver-era dreams. In the early 1870s, silver mines in the Inyo Mountains and high on the Sierra escarpment above Independence were booming. In 1874, Nevada Senator John P. Jones and local boosters chartered the Los Angeles & Independence Railroad (LA&I) with the grand plan of building a rail line from a new port at Santa Monica, through Los Angeles, over Cajon Pass, and straight up the Owens Valley to Independence. However, when rails finally reached Independence in 1883, they came from the north, not the south. Wealthy Comstock Lode financiers had built the Carson & Colorado Railway (C&C), a 3-foot / 0.9-m narrow-gauge railroad stretching 300 miles / 483 km from Mound House, Nevada (near Carson City) over Montgomery Pass and all the way down the Owens Valley. Operations in the Owens Valley ended on April 30, 1960, with the tracks torn up soon after. A 1911 Baldwin 4-6-0 narrow-gauge steam locomotive is now on display in the museum and restored to full, live-steam operating condition.

It was great listening to Mark and his stories as we wound up Onion Valley Road, one of the steepest, highest paved dead-end roads in California – climbing 5,200 vertical feet / 1,585 m in just 13 miles / 21 km. It was originally meant to be the eastern descent of a Trans-Sierra Highway that would have connected Fresno and Kings Canyon directly to Independence, but the project was stopped by wilderness advocates on March 4, 1940 when President Roosevelt signed the bill that created Kings Canyon National Park. When we reached the Onion Valley Trailhead at 9,176 ft / 2,797 m, it was still in the shadow of Independence Peak (11,631 ft / 3,545 m) to the south. There were a few cars parked, but altogether not nearly as many as last time. I said goodbye to Mark and was on my way.

Ahead of me was 2,638 ft / 804 m of ascent over the next 5 miles / 8 km to reach Kearsarge Pass (11,814 ft / 3,601 m). It was a cool 59°F / 15°C. The contrast with my descent just nineteen days earlier was staggering: instead of fleeing down the mountain in a freezing thundersnow squall battered by graupel, thunder, and blinding clouds, I was now hiking under serene blue skies. I passed the junction to Golden Trout Lake and turned south. The switchbacks were well graded and the views down the canyon gradually opened up across the sunlit floor of Owens Valley toward the distant crest of the Inyo Mountains and Winnedumah Paiute Monument (8,284 ft / 2,525 m). This 80-foot / 24-meter upright granitic monolith atop the ridge is sacred to the Owens Valley Paiute (Numa), whose legend holds that the heroic warrior Winnedumah was turned to stone while standing sentinel waiting for his fallen brother Tinemaha; early settlers also adopted the Paiute word *Inyo* – translated as "dwelling place of a great spirit" – to name both the mountain range and Inyo County.

As the trail traversed southwest across a sun-drenched granite slope dotted with coppery-orange fall brush, the shadowed north face of University Peak (13,539 ft / 4,127 m) came into view ahead. Originally called "Kearsarge Peak" by the 1864 California Geological Survey, the summit was climbed on July 12, 1896 by UC Berkeley professor Joseph N. LeConte, Helen M. Gompertz, Estelle Miller, and Belle Miller – the first women to summit a 13,000+ ft / 3,962+ m Sierra peak – who rechristened it "University Peak" in honor of the University of California, sparking a friendly collegiate rivalry with Stanford professor Bolton Coit Brown, who answered that same summer by naming nearby Mount Stanford (13,911 ft / 4,240 m) on the Kings-Kern Divide.

I continued towards Flower Lake (10,540 ft / 3,213 m) and finally took my first break on the side of the trail on the switchbacks just north of the lake. While I was having a snack, a couple with a dog approached. About 10 yards / 9 m before they reached me, the dog noticed me, got scared, and tried to turn back. The owner explained to me that it was a rescue dog and afraid of people. They tried to bribe the dog past me with treats, but with no luck. I moved higher up the side of the trail to give them more room, and eventually they had to carry the dog past me in their arms because it was too frightened. I felt bad for both the dog and the owners – and I felt even worse when I had to pass them a second time later, higher up the switchbacks. The final stretch up the pass was exhausting. Big Pothole Lake (11,300 ft / 3,444 m) lay down to my left while Mount Gould (13,005 ft / 3,964 m) towered imposingly on my right. Along the sunlit talus flank of Mount Gould, wind-sculpted whitebark pines (*Pinus albicaulis*) clung to the rocky slope. These multi-trunked timberline pines owe their distinctive clustered growth to a remarkable mutualism with Clark's nutcrackers (*Nucifraga columbiana*): because whitebark pine cones never open on their own, the birds chisel out the wingless seeds and bury up to 100,000 of them each autumn in small caches across windswept talus, and the forgotten caches sprout together as fused, multi-stemmed tree clumps.

This was now truly the next adventure. When I had turned off the PCT onto Bullfrog Lake Trail at the end of my last trip, I had been sad to leave the trail behind and promised myself I would return soon to push north over Glen Pass. Now, just three weeks later, I was back to fulfill that promise. Although I had done this stretch of the PCT in 2018 as part of the Rae Lakes Loop, I didn't remember that much. Right away it was uphill again, with several short switchbacks demanding my full physical attention. Through the trees above Vidette Meadow (9,653 ft / 2,942 m), I could look southeast up the glacially carved trough of Bubbs Creek Canyon toward the imposing granitic horn of East Vidette and the distant headwall peaks before reaching a small plateau to catch my breath.

Just past the plateau, I ran into the woman from San Diego again – she had taken the main Kearsarge Pass Trail directly to the PCT and was looping back via the Bullfrog Lake Trail.

When I reached the sun-drenched sandy bench at the junction with Kearsarge Pass Trail (10,725 ft / 3,269 m), some of my memories from 2018 came back. The warm cinnamon-barked foxtail pines (*Pinus balfouriana* subsp. *austrina*) framing this high granitic bench belong to a distinct southern Sierra subspecies isolated from its northern California relatives in the Klamath Mountains by 300 miles / 483 km; closely related to bristlecone pines, these bottle-brush-needled survivors can live for well over 1,000 to 2,000 years on dry decomposed-granite ridges. To the left was the trail down to Charlotte Lake where I had camped before. I continued straight to stay on the PCT.

The higher I climbed, the more the view down the conifer-filled Charlotte Creek valley opened up, eventually revealing the sunlit monolith of Charlotte Dome (10,690 ft / 3,258 m) at the mouth of the canyon. First climbed in October 1970 by Sierra legends Galen Rowell, Alan Steck, and Chris Vandiver, this sheer 1,200-foot / 366-meter granitic exfoliation dome features one of the most famous alpine rock routes in the range, immortalized as a centerpiece of *Fifty Classic Climbs of North America*.

Soon the trail climbed above the tree line again and turned east across the stark boulder fields south of Glen Pass. The lower tarn was already dry for the season, but the upper tarn (11,522 ft / 3,512 m) still held vivid emerald water inside its granodiorite cirque beneath a daytime waxing crescent Moon. The grade was steep, forcing me to take frequent short breaks to catch my breath.

When I finally reached the top of Glen Pass (11,978 ft / 3,651 m; 11,975 ft / 3,650 m on my GPS), I was both happy and exhausted. The pass was named in 1904 for Glen H. Crow, an early U.S. Forest Service ranger who helped lead the first recorded pack-mule train across this razor-sharp granite notch connecting the Bubbs Creek and Woods Creek watersheds. The view on the other side was spectacular: to the north, Upper Rae Lake (10,560 ft / 3,219 m) and a chain of emerald alpine tarns lay nestled below the sunlit walls of Diamond Peak (13,126 ft / 4,001 m) and Black Mountain (13,289 ft / 4,051 m), while Mount Cotter (12,616 ft / 3,845 m) and Mount Clarence King (12,907 ft / 3,934 m) rose on the left horizon. Those twin glacial horns on the King Spur honor 22-year-old geologist Clarence King (later the first director of the U.S. Geological Survey) and Richard D. Cotter, who immediately after their 1864 Mount Brewer ascent made a daring, near-fatal push across the Kings-Kern Divide armed with only a single hemp lasso and canteen – an exploit immortalized in King's classic 1872 adventure book *Mountaineering in the Sierra Nevada*. The sun was already quite low and the shadows were getting longer.

[PHOTO: 281A6712.jpg][CAPTION: Looking north from Glen Pass (11,978 ft / 3,651 m) across a chain of emerald alpine tarns (~11,279 ft / 3,438 m – 11,332 ft / 3,454 m) and a glimpse of Upper Rae Lake (10,560 ft / 3,219 m) on the right. Mount Cotter (12,616 ft / 3,845 m) and Mount Clarence King (12,907 ft / 3,934 m) rise along the King Spur on the left horizon, while sunlit Diamond Peak (13,126 ft / 4,001 m) anchors the right skyline above the Rae Lakes basin. (36°47'22.78" N 118°24'43.36" W)]

The northern side of Glen Pass was very rocky, with seemingly endless dry-laid stone switchbacks cutting across the shadowed north-facing talus slope toward the first set of tarns. To the east, Painted Lady (12,119 ft / 3,694 m) and the distant dike-streaked crest of Dragon Peak (12,885 ft / 3,927 m) looked exceptionally impressive in the low afternoon sunlight. Unlike the pale gray Cretaceous granodiorite of the surrounding peaks, Painted Lady's vibrant, multi-hued face is an ancient Mesozoic metamorphic roof pendant – a weathered raft of pre-batholithic volcanic and sedimentary rock (schist, slate, and quartzite) that capped the molten magma chamber over 100 million years ago and resisted erosion while glaciers gouged out the cirque.

Beyond the switchbacks, late-afternoon sunlight illuminates the vivid rust-orange metamorphic cliffs of Painted Lady (12,119 ft / 3,694 m) and the distant dike-streaked crest of Dragon Peak (12,885 ft / 3,927 m). Along the shadowed talus field at 11,226 ft / 3,422 m, I spotted an American pika (*Ochotona princeps*) perched alertly on a lichen-speckled granodiorite boulder right beside the trail. Because pikas do not hibernate and possess such dense fur and a high resting metabolic rate that they can fatally overheat if exposed to ambient temperatures above 78°F / 26°C, they depend on the natural air-conditioning of deep sub-talus boulder crevices in summer and spend late summer and autumn feverishly curing and stacking up to 60 lbs / 27 kg of alpine grasses and wildflowers into winter "haypiles" beneath overhanging rocks.

[PHOTO: 281A6740.jpg][CAPTION: An American pika (*Ochotona princeps*) perches alertly on a shadowed granodiorite talus boulder along the northern descent from Glen Pass (11,978 ft / 3,651 m) toward Upper Rae Lake (10,560 ft / 3,219 m), its buff throat softly lit by reflected late-afternoon sunlight off the adjacent rock face. Perfectly adapted to high-alpine boulder fields above the tree line, this pint-sized relative of rabbits and hares stays active through the winter beneath the snowpack rather than hibernating. (36°47'44.89" N 118°24'26.38" W)]

The PCT followed the western lakeshore and soon reached the junction with the Sixty Lakes Basin Trail. I had foolishly thought that I would have plenty of time to take a side trip into the Sixty Lakes Basin, but now it was just half an hour until sunset and I still needed to get to my campsite. Even though I had to skip the detour, that rugged basin just over the western ridge holds a special place in Sierra conservation history as ground zero for one of the range's greatest ecological rescues: in the late 1990s and 2000s, biologist Dr. Roland Knapp and the National Park Service launched an intensive gill-netting program to eradicate introduced non-native trout from its alpine tarns, successfully saving the endangered Sierra Nevada mountain yellow-legged frog (*Rana sierrae*) from local extinction during the catastrophic chytrid fungus epidemic. Moving on, I hopped across the boulder-strewn outlet connecting Upper and Middle Rae Lake, where a calm, shadowed cove mirrored the golden-lit peaks above.

I followed the shoreline across a small peninsula before turning north again with Middle Rae Lake on my left. A quarter mile / 0.4 km later, I reached a spur trail that took me to a large camping area on the shore of Middle Rae Lake that had a bear locker. There was room for dozens of tents, but I was the only one there for now. I didn't mind the solitude and picked a very level spot surrounded by trees.

With the sun behind the mountains, it was cooling quickly. My first objective was to filter enough water for the evening, so I took my dirty-water bag and walked down to the lake. There were no ripples on the lake surface – it was absolutely calm. I set up my tent and crawled into my sleeping bag. It was still a bit too early for dinner, so I just wanted to warm up first. For dinner I had Good To-Go Indian Vegetable Korma with the remainder of the French baguette I had brought, and it felt great to have something warm in my stomach. Before turning in, I put my water filter and water bottle into my sleeping bag to keep them from freezing and ended up falling asleep before 9pm.

Today's hike was also another milestone: I have now completed 30% of the Pacific Crest Trail. Reaching mile 794.7 / 1,278.9 km of the 2,650-mile / 4,265-km trail feels like a monumental turning point; I have now traversed the heart of the highest wilderness passes in the entire Sierra Crest, with Glen Pass behind me and the lower river valleys of Kings Canyon ahead.
