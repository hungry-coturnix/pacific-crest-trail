# Sherman Pass Road to Haiwee Pass Trail

Jul 3, 2026 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Central California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/central-california/)

**Start & End:** [36.041222, -118.134071](https://maps.app.goo.gl/C61KiKDxNsF85Tjy6)  
(a small camping area west of Kennedy Meadows Road)

**Distance:** 22.6 mi (up to PCT mile 713.5, 26.9% completed)  
**Time:** 11 hours 29 minutes (start 5:57am, end 5:27pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 4,584 ft (low 6,051 ft – high 8,604 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 4,584 ft (start 6,054 ft – end 6,054 ft)

---

I was back in Kennedy Meadows for my first official hike in the High Sierra section of the Pacific Crest Trail. My camping spot for the night was a mile north of Kennedy Meadows about 100 yards from the South Fork Kern River at the end of a dirt road spur leading to a number of riverside campsites. When I arrived at 5:20pm, there were already lots of cars and tents set up. All of them were close to the river though and since I didn’t want to enjoy mosquito heaven, I parked in a flat spot under two trees just a bit further away. The Pacific Crest Trail was a mere 50 yards away from me, intersecting the dirt road spur. My hike in the morning would be a loop starting from right here, taking the PCT north, then heading east on Haiwee Trail and south on Wildrose Trail back to my car. Sherman Pass Road – where I had left off last time – was 1.2 miles south of me, so the plan was to do an out-and-back evening stroll to the road so that I wouldn’t miss out on this stretch of the PCT.

The trail was always within earshot of the South Fork Kern River. The sagebrush across Kennedy Meadows was stretching out in front of me. Behind me to the north, I could see the narrowing mountains that the river was flowing through. The river was lined with willow thickets and the trail was quite sandy at times. After crossing a few dirt roads, I arrived at the familiar Sherman Pass Road. I headed west towards the bridge across the South Fork Kern River and enjoyed the view for a bit. It was still a strong flow. Then I headed back. The mountains were starting to cast long shadows as the sun was setting.

Back at the car, I set up the rooftop tent and had dinner. The people camped directly at the river were quite noisy, playing loud music, so I ended up using earplugs for the night. The temperature dropped into the low 40s overnight. I had fallen asleep early and when my alarm went off at 5:10am, I was well rested and ready to go. Sunrise was at 5:40am, but when I started hiking at 5:57am, everything was still in the shadows of the mountains. The PCT headed north across Kennedy Meadows, passing a few more river campsites, then crossed Kennedy Meadows Road at a river bend only to cross it again half a mile later at the entrance to Kennedy Meadows Campground. The PCT went straight through the campground, past a larger parking lot with only two cars and a big information board with a trail register right next to it. This would be the last pavement the PCT crossed for the next 200 miles. The next pavement would be in Reds Meadow near Mammoth Lakes. I signed the trail register and pushed on.

A quarter mile later, I reached the boundary of the South Sierra Wilderness. This wilderness was established by the California Wilderness Act of 1984, closing a gap between Domeland Wilderness to the south and Golden Trout Wilderness to the north. The PCT headed through pinyon pines and Jeffrey pines, paralleling the South Fork Kern River, occasionally getting close enough for direct water access. After two miles, I crossed the river via a steel-girded wooden footbridge. I would cross it again later in the day, but without a bridge. After reaching a saddle, the trail left the shaded pine canopy and entered the burn area of the 2008 Clover Fire. The fire had rapidly consumed over 13,000 acres and the area was further damaged in subsequent years by post-fire mudslides. Only a few large pine trees were still standing, and they showed clear signs of fire on their bottom half. I crossed willow lined Crag Creek, then passed along the eastern edge of Clover Meadow. The PCT continued more steeply uphill, following Crag Creek, then wound through white Common Yarrow and the bright yellow, rounded heads of Bigelow’s Sneezeweed to reach another saddle and a junction with Haiwee Trail. Shortly before the saddle, I spotted a quick flash of local wildlife: two mule deer suddenly bolted, stotting away from me – springing high into the air with all four hooves leaving the ground simultaneously to clear the brush – unfortunately too far away for any good photos.

This was the end of the PCT portion for today’s hike. However, Haiwee Trail was invisible. There was still a damaged sign that probably once had pointed to the trail, but that was all which remained. I took out my phone for some route finding, hoping that it was just the first stretch up to the base of Deer Mountain that was so heavily overgrown. The next half hour quickly degenerated into bushwhacking and backtracking. The trail was still faintly visible in some places, although you needed a keen eye to recognize the old tread beneath the brush. There were also lots of blowdowns from the aftermath of the fire. Haiwee Trail, and later on Haiwee Pass Trail, followed an ancient American Indian path to Owens Valley, but it was abundantly clear that it had not seen a single day of maintenance since the 2008 fire. Closer to the top of the plateau, the trail became easier to follow. I still lost it a few times, but my offline maps helped immensely. On top of the plateau, there were pine trees again. Apparently the fire had spared this upper portion. I crossed through an incredibly beautiful, saturated wildflower meadow carpeted with purple Sierra Lupine and the brilliant yellow globes of Bigelow’s Sneezeweed. I even spotted a massive Mountain Carpenter Bee, its bald black abdomen gleaming in the sun, nectar-robbing the lupine blossoms.

The trail headed gently downhill through the meadow, then reached the edge of the plateau. This is where the pine forest ended. Ahead of me to the east and south lay the massive, sun-baked heart of the Clover Fire burn area. The trail descended steeply, overgrown at times and not always easy to follow. I reached Dutch John Flat which was the northern edge of the burn area and still covered in pine trees. Now paralleling an unnamed creek, I had to hike around large willow thickets that had grown across the trail – or what little remained of it. The junction with Deer Mountain Trail still had a sign, but that trail was even more invisible than Haiwee Trail. When I finally reached the South Fork Kern River, I was hoping that the route finding would come to an end. The river was still more than 10 feet wide here and flowing swiftly. I took off my shoes and socks and started to cross. But the submerged river rocks were coated in a slick layer of green algae and it was tough to get a firm grip. I chose to backtrack to the bank, put my shoes back on, and wade through. Crossing with shoes gave me the much-needed traction and I reached the southern bank safely without any slips.

I filtered some fresh water and took another break, then turned south onto Wildrose Trail. It was immediately uphill via switchbacks, but at least the trail was clearly visible, albeit with lots of pine cones on it. The ascent was short-lived and the trail headed back down, crossed a dry creek bed, then followed another dry wash uphill. The pine trees abruptly vanished once more as I entered the burn area of the Clover Fire again. I would stay in the burn area for the next 5 miles. The trail became exceptionally difficult to find in sections where it crossed regrown, brushy flats or passed directly through choked willow thickets. It was slightly easier when the trail dropped into the sandy bottom of dry creek beds, but I still had to make several detours around dense brush blockades. On top of the physical navigation, there was zero shade cover and the afternoon sun was burning relentlessly. Fortunately, the temperature was just in the low 80s. A mile north of Wildrose Meadow, I finally stepped back into the welcome shade of an unburned pine forest. The trail returned to a sandy creek bed that was easy to follow. Crossing Wildrose Meadow, however, required another round of bushwhacking. Past the meadow, the trail headed uphill again in another dry creek bed to a final saddle, then downhill for most of the remaining way. I still lost the trail twice more, but was able to backtrack quickly.

Close to Kennedy Meadows, finding the trail stopped being an issue, but the trail tread was covered in loose rocks and heavy pine cones that required cautious stepping on tired ankles. Eventually, the trail widened into a dirt road which led me straight back to the paved Kennedy Meadows Road. I turned north for 100 yards, then took the spur trail to the riverside camping area where my car was waiting. I had not anticipated that much trail finding and bushwhacking today. I was glad I had managed to complete the loop in a reasonable time. But I was also a bit worried that I would have to do at least Haiwee Trail again next time when I would do another loop starting at Olancha Pass Trailhead.

---

**Trail Conditions:** The PCT is mostly well-maintained dirt singletrack with some fairly sandy stretches along the South Fork Kern River within Kennedy Meadows. Haiwee Trail and Wildrose Trail have not see any maintenance in decades. Both trails are largely within the area burned by the 2008 Clover Fire. The path is both heavily overgrown and very hard to find in most places requiring significant bushwhacking. Both trails are also very exposed to the sun as trees have not grown back since the fire.

**Parking:** There is plenty of room for cars in dirt pullouts between the South Fork Kern River and the Pacific Crest Trail.

**Crowds:** Very lightly traveled. Only met one backpacker going southbound on the Pacific Crest Trail. Didn’t meet anyone on Haiwee Trail and Wildrose Trail.

**Weather:** Sunny with no clouds. Light intermittent breeze past noon. Low of 45°F / 7°C in the morning, at the beginning of the hike near South Fork Kern River. High of 88°F / 31°C in the late afternoon back at Kennedy Meadows. Humidity between 52% in the morning and 15% in the afternoon.

**Land Manager & Permits:** South Sierra Wilderness. Sequoia National Forest and Inyo National Forest. No permit required.

**Overall Rating:** 3.1

Difficulty: Very Strenuous
