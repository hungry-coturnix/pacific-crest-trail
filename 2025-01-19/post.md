# Lost Valley Road to Tule Canyon Truck Trail

Jan 19, 2025 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Southern California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/southern-california/)

**Start:** [33.325990, -116.636930](https://maps.app.goo.gl/jwo4MRjerg6UDYHf8) (Unnamed trailhead on Lost Valley Road)  
**End:** [33.467025, -116.622491](https://maps.app.goo.gl/vgmYy2ujFGRhBW9EA) (Tule Canyon Truck Trail)

**Distance:** 22.2 mi (up to PCT mile 137.0, 5.2% completed)  
**Time:** 9 hours 37 minutes (start 7:02am, end 4:39pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 3,986 ft (low 3,608 ft – high 6,238 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 3,804 ft (start 3,916 ft – end 4,098 ft)

---

I can confidently say that today was the most physically exhausting day I have ever had in my entire life. And on top of that, the day ended with me in the back of a police car (spoiler alert: I was not arrested).

Let me start at the very beginning. I knew ahead of time that today would be logistically challenging. There are 34 miles of PCT between the Lost Valley Road and CA-74. Obviously too much for a day hike. Backpacking proved equally difficult. Water halfway along the trail is at Tule Spring and has been reported as either dry or contaminated over the last few years. Splitting this section into two day hikes however came with other logistical challenges as there were very few access points. I had originally planned to use a dirt road at mile 134.5 and use a dirt track south of Beauty Mountain Wilderness to cycle back.

The evening before the hike, I was more closely looking at satellite images again and realized that the first dirt road was private and both roads seemed to have a closed gate. The alternative was to go via Tule Canyon Truck Trail. However, this was an unmaintained dirt track and it was not clear how much of that I would be able to drive with my low clearance car. In addition to that, I would have to go north around Beauty Mountain Wilderness on the way back which would add at least 5 miles to the bike ride. Absent any better options, I settled on this plan.

I left the hotel at 5:06am. Tule Canyon Truck Trail was indeed not in great condition. While initially still manageable, it became sketchy after 0.8 miles, so I decided to leave the bike there and chained it to one of the bigger bushes along the dirt road, just past a large abandoned trailer. It was still about 1.7 miles to the intersection with the PCT, so that would prolong today’s hike even more. Still optimistic, I left at 5:44am for the 50 mile car ride to the starting point.

![Sunrise over Warner Valley (33°19’36.378″ N 116°38’13.566″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/281A4400-DeNoiseAI-standard-1024x683.jpg)

Sunrise was at 6:49am and I arrived at 6:51am, energized to get going. It was still cold at 43°F but the gentle elevation gain warmed me up pretty quickly. I stopped briefly after about 2 miles to just take in the scenery. Lake Henshaw was “steaming” in the distance and sunlight was slowly covering the canyons and valleys in front of me. It was a serenely peaceful moment. There was absolutely no sound, no wind, no birds, it was incredibly quiet. So much so that I could hear my own heartbeat very clearly.

![A rocky canyon east of Chihuahua Valley (33°21’33.09″ N 116°36’25.044″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/281A4431-DeNoiseAI-standard-1024x683.jpg)

![Chihuahua Valley (33°22’6.09″ N 116°36’9.6″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/281A4440-DeNoiseAI-standard-1024x683.jpg)

The trail was bending eastward, slowly revealing views of Chihuahua Valley to the north. I took my first break at 5 miles overlooking a small green canyon to the southeast. Turning north again, the scenery became more rocky and the trail alternated between ups and downs. I crossed Lost Valley Road at 9.6 miles with the highlight of today’s hike in sight: Combs Peak. The PCT passed below the peak over a saddle, but a short spur trail led to the summit. The trail was very steep and very overgrown, which made progress slow. The views from the top of Combs Peak were worth it though. A 360 degree panorama: Chihuahua Valley to the west, Terwilliger Valley and Anza to the north, the Santa Rosa Mountain Range to the east, and Hot Springs Mountain to the south.

I stayed at the peak for a bit just to soak in the view. Two hikers that I had passed on the way reached the top just before I was going to leave. The couple was visiting from Tahoe and we chatted a bit about hiking, the view, and how dry it was. I made my way down the peak to continue north on the PCT. It was mostly downhill with a gentle gradient. Views of Terwilliger Valley to the north were dominating the view. I finally left San Diego County and crossed into Riverside County. Soon after, I reached the private dirt road I had originally planned to use as the exit and I could confirm by its conditions that no car had been there in a long time.

![Descending from Combs Peak towards Terwilliger Valley (33°24’10.968″ N 116°36’24.168″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/281A4482-DeNoiseAI-standard-1024x683.jpg)

![Santa Rosa Mountains (Santa Rosa Mountains)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/281A4486-DeNoiseAI-standard-1024x683.jpg)

The trail started to flatten out. At about 20 miles, I reached Tule Canyon. It was dry now, but it was obvious that it could be a strong stream after heavy rain. The PCT headed up on the northern side of the canyon and soon crossed Tule Canyon Truck Trail. This is where I left the PCT and headed west. The trail was rough, even for a high clearance 4WD. Another half mile later, I crossed a gate marking the boundary of Anza-Borrego Desert State Park. The sun was already close to the horizon when I finally reached my bike at 4:39pm.

**Trail Conditions:** Mostly dirt with some shorter rocky stretches, somewhat overgrown past Combs Peak.

**Parking:** There is parking for a few cars in two pullouts along the Lost Valley Road.

**Weather:** Sunny throughout the day with a high of 78°F (around 2:30pm, at the boundary line between San Diego County and Riverside County) and a low of 40°F (early in the morning, at the start of the hike).

**Land Manager & Permits:** Cleveland National Forest and Anza-Borrego Desert State Park. No permits required.

**Overall Rating:** 2.8

Difficulty: Moderate  
Scenery: Great  
Logistics: Very Difficult

---

**Bike Shuttle:** 25.4 mi (5 hours 41 min), 2,451 ft elevation gain, 4.47 mph average speed

Needless to say that I had expected to do most of the cycling during daylight hours. Sunset was at 5:06pm, so that left me with about 45 minutes until full darkness. It was clear to me at this point that a significant portion of the bike ride back would be in the dark. But I didn’t really have a choice, so I decided not to fret about it and get going. The temperature had already dropped to about 40°F, but I still felt reasonably warm with my light jacket.

I followed Tule Canyon Truck Trail to the end and then turned north onto Terwilliger Road, a dirt road in very good condition. When I passed one of the properties on the right, several large dogs came running at me barking very loudly which scared me for a second and kept me paddling faster so that they wouldn’t be able to catch up to me. Turning left on Ramsey Road and right on Chapman Road, I was fortunate to get a stretch of paved road, albeit somewhat uphill, which allowed me to go a little faster. Turning left on Bailey Road, this was truly the end of any pavement for the rest of the way.

Stretches of Bailey Road were very sandy forcing me to get off my bike and just push it. And even when there was less sand, the elevation gain still had me on my feet and pushing the bike. When I reached the top of the hill, there was very little daylight left and I turned on my headlamp. I had to point almost right at my front tire so that I could see exactly what was in front of me (holes? rocks?) but that also meant I really couldn’t ride very fast – not that I had so much energy left to do that anyway.

The way was somewhat flat for a bit as I turned south and followed several turns to reach Cooper Cienega Truck Trail. Long stretches uphill, sometimes very steep, had me pushing my bike for an extended period of time. It was also getting colder – still manageable, but I couldn’t say I felt particularly warm. It was absolutely dark at this point, with no light at all. Of course this wasn’t really a surprise since it was within a designated wilderness. I looked at the offline map on my phone regularly to make sure that I was still on the right path, but progress seemed extraordinarily slow and I started to get worried. It was still a very long way back to the car, the hours kept passing by, my neck had started to hurt from staring at the exact spot in front of me all the time, and I was afraid my headlamp would eventually run out of battery. I stopped a few times to get some rest and turned off the headlamp to save energy and I could see a gazillion stars in the sky above and even the faint outline of the milky way, except that I couldn’t fully appreciate it in the moment.

Even on the downhill stretches I was not able to ride very fast. It was steep at times and even when it wasn’t, there were so many loose rocks on the ground that I had to navigate very carefully and stay on breaks for most of the time. Around 8pm, more than 3 hours into the ride, I finally started seeing lights from some of the houses in Chihuahua Valley which lifted my spirit a tiny bit, even though I knew it was still a long way to go. I finally crossed paved Chihuahua Valley Road and looked at my phone again for the next turns. The battery was down to 18% but as I looked at it, I could see the battery percentage going down rapidly and within 5 seconds the phone shut down.

So now my phone was dead. I was very exhausted. It was cold. And I still had about 7 miles to go. I knew there was on more left turn onto Lost Valley Road and I was hoping that would be all to worry about. The road in front of me had plenty of sand again, so I alternated between riding and pushing my bike. The dirt road eventually turned left and I was confident that this was the turn I needed to take. From here, it was a long way downhill. The miles kept on stretching and stretching and I was getting worried again how long it would take me to get back to my car or at least past the campground at which point the dirt road would turn into a paved road. I eventually saw some lights and thought this might be the campground, though the number of lights somewhat surprised me for a campground.

I passed a gate and then a sign that said this was Warner Springs Estates, a private property. At this point I knew I had not taken the correct left turn and instead gone down Puerta La Cruz Road. Several things went through my head at the same time. For one, I was relieved that I knew where I was and it was clear I was close to CA-79, a paved highway. On the other hand, it also dawned on me that in order to get back to my car, I would have to ride the highway to Warner Springs and then more than 4 miles uphill on Lost Valley Road with more than 1,000 feet of elevation gain. It was past 10pm and this would take me at least another 2 hours, should I physically still be able to do that – which seemed more uncertain by the minute.

I started riding south on CA-79. It was paved, so I could ride fast, but the cold air was biting and my hands – despite wearing cycling gloves – were blocks of ice within minutes. Looking at my temperature recordings later on, it had dropped to 26°F at that point and I was still only wearing a light jacket. I passed a sign that showed Warner Springs about 8 miles away. This meant 7 miles on the highway before the 4 miles uphill on Lost Valley Road. I was starting to doubt myself.

Then I passed one of these blue call boxes that are typically along highways. Initially my pride was in the way and I kept riding. By the second call box, I started to seriously think about whether I should use it. It was a call box, not an emergency box after all. And I didn’t consider myself in an emergency situation quite yet. I was not doing great at all, but an emergency was still some time away. At the third call box (I was so lucky that there were 3 call boxes on that 7 mile stretch!) I finally stopped and picked up the phone. It was 10:30pm.

The operator asked me for my name and the call box number and why I was calling. Then they connected me to the California Highway Patrol (CHP). I was not quite expecting that, but then I don’t know what the alternative would have been. They repeatedly asked me whether I needed an ambulance, but I insisted that I just needed a ride back to my car. Unfortunately, there was no officer working at the time, so they had to call the officer on duty to come and get me. I felt a little bad for disturbing the officer’s peaceful Sunday evening with such a request, but I really needed that ride. They couldn’t give me an ETA and I wasn’t expecting it to be that fast given how remote I was.

As I was waiting in the middle of nowhere, trying to keep myself warm, I was reflecting on what I had learned from this experience:

* If the PCT access is via dirt roads, make sure they are accessible and not private. Don’t ignore the fact that OpenStreetMap can’t route there.
* Riding on dirt roads in darkness is a bad idea. It’s very easy to miss a turn and the risk of accidents is much higher.
* Your pace on dirt roads will be much slower than on paved roads, especially when there is substantial elevation gain involved.

Today’s bike ride back should have been a shuttle ride. If I had not missed the last turn, I might have been able to get back to my car on my own, but even then it would have been an ordeal. 25 miles of bike ride after a 22 mile hike is bordering what’s humanly possible.

The CHP officer finally arrived at 11:20pm. He was very friendly and even offered to put my bike on his bumper. That didn’t work out, but I appreciated the gesture. He drove me up Lost Valley Road and I eventually got back to my car at 11:44pm. I picked up my bike and drove to Temecula to find a hotel for the night and take a hot shower. It was past 1:30am when I could finally fall asleep.
