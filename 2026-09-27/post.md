Cottonwood Pass to Bullfrog Lake Trail (Day 2)

Start: 36.551884, -118.358122 (Lower Crabtree Meadow)
End: 36.730716, -118.373061 (Upper Bubbs Creek Canyon)

Distance: 19.1 mi / 30.7 km (up to PCT mile 785.2, 29.6% completed)
Time: 10 hours 33 minutes (start 7:28am, end 6:01pm)
Elevation Gain: 4,669 ft / 1,423 m (low 10,327 ft / 3,148 m – high 13,153 ft / 4,009 m)
Elevation Loss: 4,467 ft / 1,362 m (start 10,334 ft / 3,150 m – end 10,536 ft / 3,211 m)

----------

When my alarm went off at 6:20am, it had just started dawning and sunrise was still half an hour away. I had slept better than expected, waking up a few times through the night, but it had been cold. To keep my water filter and water bottle from freezing, I had kept both inside my sleeping bag. The couple that had camped about 100 yards / 91 m from me was already out and about, breaking camp. I didn't want to leave the warmth of my sleeping bag, but I knew I just had to get it over with. Today was going to be another long day that would get me across the highest point on the Pacific Crest Trail, Forester Pass (13,153 ft / 4,009 m), and from Sequoia National Park into Kings Canyon National Park. The weather looked perfect and I was anticipating lots of epic views along the way. Just as I crawled out of my tent, the couple was leaving. They told me that they had summited Mount Whitney the day before and were now heading south. I shared with them that I had not won a permit in the lottery for Mount Whitney this year and was heading north.

I had left my clean water bag with the rest of the filtered water outside. Now it was frozen. Not a solid block of ice just yet, but frozen enough that I couldn't pour it into the pot to boil. I still had enough in my water bottle for breakfast, and worst case, I always could have filtered some fresh water from nearby Crabtree Creek. Breakfast was Peak Refuel Mountain Berry Granola and a cup of hot Jasmine tea. The tea felt particularly good, warming me up from the inside as I was breaking camp. I was ready to leave by 7:28am, double checking that I had packed everything. The sun was up by now, although not quite yet reaching over the mountains to the east. The very top of Mount Young (13,176 ft / 4,016 m) was already illuminated, but from my side, Mount Whitney (14,505 ft / 4,421 m) was still in the dark. About 100 yards / 91 m away was Whitney Creek after it had merged with Crabtree Creek. I hopped over the rocks and crossed safely without getting my shoes wet. To the east was a spur trail that led to the Crabtree Ranger Station and then further to Mount Whitney, but I headed north to continue on the Pacific Crest Trail.

It was uphill straight away. Over about 0.6 mi / 1.0 km, the trail gained almost 500 ft / 152 m of elevation, warming me up from the inside. Then I reached the junction with the John Muir Trail (JMT). The JMT shares some of its route with the PCT and here it was turning east towards Mount Whitney. This also meant that I had reached the end of the California Section G of the Pacific Crest Trail which had taken me 115 mi / 185 km from Walker Pass in the desert through Kennedy Meadows and into the High Sierra. Section H would lead me 175.5 mi / 282.4 km to Tuolumne Meadows in Yosemite National Park. I was excited about this new milestone and continued.

The trail passed along Sandy Meadow, revealing far-reaching views to the Great Western Divide at the heart of Sequoia National Park and Mount Kaweah (13,802 ft / 4,207 m). Morning light illuminated the golden autumn grasses of the meadow, casting long shadows from the surrounding foxtail and lodgepole pines, with the pale granodiorite crest of Mount Kaweah gleaming on the western horizon.

From the saddle, I could see the bare face of Mount Barnard (13,990 ft / 4,264 m) in the distance. The peak was named in 1892 by the Sierra Club – specifically Joseph N. LeConte – in honor of Edward Emerson Barnard, an illustrious astronomer at Lick and Yerkes Observatories who that same year discovered Jupiter's fifth moon (Amalthea) and later identified Barnard's Star. Now it was downhill again to Wallace Creek. The creek had carved a deeply incised canyon over the ages. The subalpine willows along the trail had put on bright fall colors already, glowing brilliant yellow and gold against the granitic slopes.

A backpacker was coming up from Wallace Creek, certainly younger than I am, and instead of a good morning greeting, he just told me that he was so tired. Then he sarcastically added that he was looking forward to summiting Mount Whitney. I felt sad for the guy, but I didn't want to crush his confidence any further.

I chatted with another couple passing me and they told me that the forecast for the following night was 1°F / -17°C with snow above 8,500 ft / 2,591 m. I was a little shocked to hear that and it seemed somewhat implausible, although I forgot to ask which elevation that temperature was for. Either way, I would be off trail by tomorrow afternoon, so this shouldn't affect me. At Wallace Creek, I took a short break and filtered some water. The creek was named for Judge William B. Wallace (1849 – 1932) of Visalia, an avid early Sierra explorer and charter member of the Sierra Club who played a pivotal role in lobbying Congress to establish Sequoia National Park in 1890. The water sources were plentiful enough now that I only had to carry half a liter / 17 fl oz in between, which was good news for the weight on my back.

On the other side of Wallace Creek was a major trail junction connecting with the High Sierra Trail (HST), which crosses the Great Western Divide over Kaweah Gap all the way from Crescent Meadow, as well as routes leading down the North Fork Kern River and deeper into Sequoia National Park. This is one of the most storied backcountry crossroads in the entire range. I continued north, but it was steeply uphill again, out of the canyon that Wallace Creek had carved. The trail contoured through an open subalpine foxtail pine forest along the north wall of the canyon, looking back across the incised drainage toward the broad granite massif of Peak 12,585 ft / 3,836 m.

I passed an older Chinese gentleman who was on a trip with his three brothers up ahead. They were on the same route that I was although with shorter distances every day. I told him about the weather forecast I had heard about and he was immediately concerned. He asked me to share the news with his brothers as well who were ahead of him. His pace was significantly slower than mine, so I moved along and left him behind.

About half a mile / 0.8 km later, I reached Bighorn Plateau (~11,400 ft / 3,475 m). I had seen this place in some of the YouTube videos of the PCT thru-hikers that I am following this year, but the videos had obviously been only a pale reflection of reality. Like the Siberian Outpost from yesterday, this vast, treeless upland is an ancient Tertiary erosion surface – an elevated relict peneplain that escaped the gouging of ice age glaciers while Wallace and Tyndall Creeks carved deep canyons on either side. The plateau takes its name from the endangered Sierra Nevada bighorn sheep (*Ovis canadensis sierrae*), whose Mount Williamson and Mount Baxter herds inhabit the rugged crags and fellfields surrounding this tableland. Across the expansive, gravelly fellfields, the jagged Sierra Crest commanded the horizon with the broad granite shoulders of Mount Tyndall (14,019 ft / 4,273 m) and Mount Williamson (14,375 ft / 4,382 m) rising in the northeast, while the rounded summit of Tawny Point (12,317 ft / 3,754 m) anchored the foreground.

I wanted to stop every 10 steps and take another photo. It was just breathtaking. Bighorn Lake was right on the plateau, but very shallow, resting peacefully amidst the golden alpine turf and reflecting the brilliant blue sky against the distant backdrop of the Great Western Divide.

Before reaching the creek, I passed the junction with Shepherd Pass Trail leading northeast to the desert floor. I had considered this as an exit for a PCT section, but it was more than 17 mi / 27 km from here to the trailhead making it a full day worth of hiking and much inferior to my current plan. After crossing Tyndall Creek which was flowing strong, I took another break and filtered some more water. This would need to get me over Forester Pass. It was 12:30pm now and I still had quite a bit of distance to cover today. On top of that, the air would have less oxygen over Forester Pass which was going to slow me down – how much was yet to be seen. The four brothers arrived shortly before I left, they were changing plans and turning around.

From Tyndall Creek it was about 2,300 ft / 701 m uphill to Forester Pass. Just past the creek crossing, a weathered wooden trail sign marked the junction for Lake South America and the Kern River Trail at 11,062 ft / 3,372 m, with the distant ramparts of the Kings-Kern Divide rising ahead.

Even though it was very sunny, it was still cold, barely above 50°F / 10°C and it would get colder with elevation. I settled into a steady rhythm, stopping occasionally to take a photo. First summited in 1864 by Clarence King and Richard Cotter during the California Geological Survey, the peak was named in honor of British physicist and mountaineer John Tyndall, famous for experimentally proving the greenhouse effect of atmospheric gases and explaining why the sky is blue.

Now I was at the base of the final ascent to Forester Pass. The last 0.8 mi / 1.3 km were switchbacks blasted and carved into the near-vertical granite slope, pushing me up the final 700 ft / 213 m of elevation. Before this spectacular cliffside shelf was blasted open in 1929 – 1930, early John Muir Trail hikers and pack trains had to cross over Junction Pass (13,220 ft / 4,029 m) a mile to the north, which was notorious for dangerously loose scree and deadly animal falls. The new route was named Forester Pass to honor the U.S. Forest Service rangers, led by Jesse Hunter, who discovered and surveyed this lower notch through the divide. Completed in 1930 as a joint engineering triumph between the Forest Service and the National Park Service, constructing the trail was perilous work; a bronze plaque bolted to the granite cliff on this south approach commemorates 18-year-old crewman Donald Downs, who was tragically killed here by a rockfall during construction. The switchbacks were steep and I could really feel the elevation now. I started counting steps and had to take a short break every 50 steps. Sometimes, I didn't even make it to 50. The views south were insanely breathtaking though. At 12,654 ft / 3,857 m on the cliffside switchbacks, a sweeping look-back panorama opened south over the upper tarns, Diamond Mesa, and Mount Tyndall.

The final switchbacks took every last bit of energy out of me. But then I reached Forester Pass. I had made it to the highest point along the Pacific Crest Trail at 13,153 ft / 4,009 m. This was definitely an accomplishment. The pass also marked the boundary between Sequoia National Park and Kings Canyon National Park with a historic wooden boundary sign placed right in the notch. The notch cuts through the formidable Kings-Kern Divide, the grand transverse granite barrier connecting Junction Peak (13,888 ft / 4,233 m) on the Sierra Crest with the Great Western Divide, separating the Kern River watershed flowing south toward the ancient Tulare Lake basin from the Kings River drainage flowing north. Junction Peak serves as the keystone of the entire region: named in 1892 by the Whitney Survey and Joseph N. LeConte because it marks the critical geographic junction where the transverse divide intersects the main Sierra Crest, it forms the triple-point boundary between Sequoia National Park, Kings Canyon National Park, and Inyo National Forest, as well as the hydrologic divide between the Kern, Kings, and Owens River basins.

North of the pass, I could see Center Peak (12,760 ft / 3,889 m) and the ridge behind with Mount Bradley (13,289 ft / 4,051 m) and University Peak (13,632 ft / 4,155 m). Both peaks carry academic heritage: University Peak was named in 1896 in honor of UC Berkeley, while Mount Bradley honors Cornelius Beach Bradley, a UC Berkeley English professor and charter member of the Sierra Club. Some 900 ft / 274 m below was a large tarn fed by the abundant snow that this area got every winter. I could also see the numerous switchbacks that would get me back down.

I had two options now. Camp above tree line at one of the tarns along the way that I could see from up here. Or make it all the way down into the forest to where Bubbs Creek was coming down from the Center Basin Canyon. Bubbs Creek takes its name from John Bubbs, an 1864 prospector and stockman who crossed Kearsarge Pass from Owens Valley during the mining boom. Pushing all the way down would not only shelter me from the biting winds, but it would also position me well for tomorrow's final climb over Kearsarge Pass to Onion Valley, where Chris and I had staged my car under the bright Harvest Moon on Friday night. I was worried that the temperature at night at a higher elevation without tree coverage was going to be very low, so I decided to go for the second option, even though that added 2 more mi / 3.2 km to the day.

The PCT switchbacked to reach the big tarn I had seen from Forester Pass. Looking back from the cirque basin at 12,326 ft / 3,757 m across the cobalt tarn, the dramatic pyramidal horn of Junction Peak (13,888 ft / 4,233 m) towered majestically along the Kings-Kern Divide.

I moved below tree line and finally reached Bubbs Creek. Next to the creek was a larger area that could accommodate at least 10 tents. But I was alone today. When I put down my backpack, there was only half an hour of daylight left and I was tired from yet another very exhausting day. I filtered water from the nearby creek and set up my tent. Dinner was Farm to Summit Garden Mac & Cheese. This time I had enough fuel for my burner, so the pasta was al dente and tasted better than the first time. Darkness had settled by the time I finished dinner and I quickly retreated to my sleeping bag. The temperature had dropped quickly after sundown. I was reflecting on the day: The views had been out of this world, rewarding me with the most stunning panoramas nature can offer. Forester Pass had kicked my ass, but it had also offered me an incredible high country experience. Both Sequoia National Park and Kings Canyon National Park encompassed some truly impressive landscapes. Sleep came quickly, recharging me for the last day on this trip.

Trail Conditions: The Pacific Crest Trail was in good condition and easy to follow throughout the whole day. Some sections were quite sandy, other sections were extremely rocky, especially around Forester Pass.

Crowds: Lightly traveled. Only met 24 backpackers throughout the day as well as 5 horses with their handler. When I camped, I was all by myself.

Wildlife: There were plenty of chipmunks and ground squirrels throughout the day as well as a yellow-bellied marmot (*Marmota flaviventris*) on the final ascent to Forester Pass.

Weather: Mostly sunny with some scattered clouds throughout the first half of the day. The clouds had dissipated by mid-afternoon. Low of 30°F / -1°C in the morning at the beginning of the hike. High of 55°F / 13°C along a sheltered stretch south of Tyndall Creek around noon.

Land Manager & Permits: Sequoia National Park. Kings Canyon National Park. Wilderness permit required (no quota).
