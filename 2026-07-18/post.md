# Haiwee Pass Trail to Olancha Pass Trail (Day 1)

Jul 18, 2026 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Central California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/central-california/)

**Start:** [36.225605, -118.061429](https://maps.app.goo.gl/H5We7V8h4uiEUFuB6) (Olancha Pass Trailhead)  
**End:** [36.139569, -118.129383](https://maps.app.goo.gl/hrFrby2bkp7kNnv8A) (Meadow along Haiwee Pass Trail)

**Distance:** 16.5 mi (up to PCT mile 713.5, 26.9% completed)  
**Time:** 12 hours 43 minutes (start 6:08am, end 6:58pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 5,643 ft (low 5,841 ft – high 9,337 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 2,887 ft (start 5,841 ft – end 8,597 ft)

---

The High Sierra was really in front of me now. It was crazy how quickly the landscape had changed. Within a mere four hikes, I had gone from barren desert with scorching heat to shaded pine forests and meadows with much more moderate temperatures. I had yet to see the first lake, but I knew it wouldn’t be far away – Chicken Spring Lake near Cottonwood Pass, about 38.5 miles further along the Pacific Crest Trail. But being in the High Sierra in a long stretch of wilderness also meant that day hikes were not an option anymore. Just getting to the PCT would often be half a day’s hike, so most trips ahead would be backpacking with one or two nights in the tent. Access to the PCT was usually from the east, from the high desert floor, which also meant a lot of elevation gain right from the start. Nonetheless, I was very excited to be in one of the most spectacular sections of the Pacific Crest Trail.

![Sunrise over Olancha Pass Trailhead, view from my roof top tent (36°13’31.9499″ N 118°3’41.1735″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4162-1024x683.jpg)

![The first view across the high desert towards the mountains in the east (36°13’35.2069″ N 118°3’43.8862″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4172-1024x683.jpg)

Today’s trip would lead me into the heart of the South Sierra Wilderness within Inyo National Forest. The plan was to start at Olancha Pass Trailhead on the eastern side of the Sierra Nevada, head up to Olancha Pass and then turn south on Honey Bee Trail until I reached Haiwee Pass Trail. I would cross the South Fork Kern River in the same spot as last time and then camp along Haiwee Pass Trail at the beautiful wildflower meadow I had seen at the base of Deer Mountain. On the next day, I would finally reach the PCT again and hike north through Monache Meadow, cross the South Fork Kern River again, hike past Summit Meadow, and exit via Olancha Pass. My only worry was whether Olancha Pass Trail and Honey Bee Trail would be in the same poor conditions as I had found Haiwee Pass Trail last time. That would significantly slow me down, especially with a heavier backpack. Long story short, it did end up slowing me down, but it was not quite as bad as last time.

![View towards Olancha (36°13’36.0317″ N 118°3’47.7059″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4174-1024x683.jpg)

![Sage Flat (36°13’37.41″ N 118°3’50.358″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4176-1024x683.jpg)

The last mile to Olancha Pass Trailhead was unpaved, but the road was well maintained. When I arrived around 8:30pm, it was still a sweltering 91°F / 33°C. An old cattle enclosure was nearby, long unused. I found a fairly level spot to park, but the moment I stepped out of the car, a swarm of hungry mosquitoes was waiting. I quickly set up my roof top tent and retreated inside. The temperature barely dropped overnight, I slept without a sleeping bag, using it only as a loose blanket. When my alarm went off at 5:10am, it was still 75°F / 24°C, but at least the mosquitoes had dispersed. I packed up, double checked that I had everything in my backpack, then set out shortly after 6am. The sun had come over the mountains to the east and was starting to paint everything in warm colors.

![Up the canyon towards Olancha Pass (36°13’27.6258″ N 118°4’34.0825″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4193-1024x683.jpg)

![Stunning views east climbing up from the high desert floor (36°13’16.4899″ N 118°5’0.8437″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4209-1024x683.jpg)

As anticipated, the ascent was immediate and steady. Over the next 5 miles, I climbed 3,400 feet / 1,000 meters all the way to Olancha Pass. The trail was in reasonably good condition, somewhat sandy at times and often littered with loose gravel and rocks, but overall not difficult to follow and not badly overgrown. It headed west up a canyon, then started switchbacking up the north side of the canyon to gain more elevation. Looking back east offered grand views over the high desert near Olancha and the dry basin of Owens Lake. The temperature remained relatively stable, it was warming up quickly but I was also gaining elevation equally quickly. I took a break under a few pine trees, then resumed the relentless ascent. After about 4 miles, the trail crossed a saddle, then headed straight west, still uphill. Close to the pass, a creek was still flowing across the trail. This could be a good spot to filter water on the way back. I was elated when I finally reached Olancha Pass after 5 miles. The views were not as far reaching as I had hoped, but they were certainly not bad. A weathered wooden post stood right at the crest, bearing a wooden arrow carved with “Haiwee Pass” to guide the way.

![Approaching Olancha Pass (36°12’43.4931″ N 118°6’15.2083″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4222-1024x683.jpg)

![The junction with Honey Bee Trail (36°12’43.08″ N 118°6’16.692″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4225-1024x683.jpg)

Turning left onto Honey Bee Trail, the ascent continued, but the trail was much more faintly visible and considerably overgrown. I lost the trail a few times and had to use my offline map. Especially areas choked with tall grass were particularly tricky as there was little indication, as all signs of former foot traffic were buried. After another rest break in the shade of a pine canopy, the trail broke out into a wide, exposed sagebrush expanse. To the left, sweeping views opened up toward the distant Coso Range Wilderness. Straight ahead stood 9,884 feet tall Round Mountain. The trail veered to the right, staying above a broader sagebrush canyon. The path was practically invisible, I was reading faint terrain clues just to guess the route through the endless sagebrush. Pushing through heavy brush with a full backpack made for slow, exhausting work. Underneath a few solitary pine trees, I stopped for a long, much-needed lunch break. The views were great, but the heat on these shadeless flats was thoroughly draining. On top of that, there had been lots of cheatgrass along the trail and its dry bristles with its microscopic backward-facing barbs had punctured my socks everywhere and were giving me a hard time.

![The last glimpse of the desert floor (36°12’14.2374″ N 118°5’43.0097″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4239-1024x683.jpg)

![Round Mountain (36°12’12.9985″ N 118°5’37.0326″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4243-1024x683.jpg)

I had a [Good-to-Go Cuban Rice Bowl](https://goodto-go.com/products/cuban-rice-bowl) for lunch. I was already running a bit low on water, but I figured I would reach the South Fork Kern River soon to filter some more. While the food was rehydrating, I dozed a little in the pleasant shade. Somewhat recovered, I continued my journey south on Honey Bee Trail. The trail eventually moved into a dry creek bed with an ocean of yellow and white wildflowers, then headed up another saddle just before reaching Haiwee Pass Trail. It was less than a mile to the river now and I was very thirsty. The heat was parching. I passed a rocky outcrop with a spectacular view of the Kern River canyon. Stepping up to the edge of the granite rim, I looked straight down into the notch where the dark ribbon of the South Fork Kern River flowed through the canyon floor. I hurried steeply down to the river. I dropped my backpack on a large boulder and fetched some water for filtering. There wouldn’t be any water near my campsite tonight (which is called dry camping) so my plan was to carry about 3 liters / 0.8 gallons to drink and for cooking, both for the rest of the day and the morning. I gulped down the fourth liter once the water was filtered.

![South Fork Kern River valley in the distance (36°10’55.1604″ N 118°5’6.8523″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4263-1024x683.jpg)

![Descending towards Haiwee Pass Trail (36°9’46.962″ N 118°4’54.924″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4276-1024x683.jpg)

Having learned my lesson from the previous trip, I had brought my water shoes this time. Switching shoes made navigating the algae-slick river stones fast and safe. The river crossing was exactly as I remembered it from last time, but it was much easier with the right shoes. I sat down on the other side and changed back into my regular hiking shoes. Then it was uphill again for the final leg of the day. Ahead was Dutch John Flat and the overgrown and faintly visible Haiwee Pass Trail. Even though I knew the area now, I still lost the trail quite a few times and had to use my offline map. The last 2 miles were significantly uphill again to the meadow at the base of Deer Mountain. A welcome scattering of afternoon clouds offered intermittent shade, but carrying 3 extra liters of water for 2 miles on exhausted legs made every step a grind. Reaching the top of the plateau was absolute bliss, the gradient finally leveled off, pine canopy provided cool shade, and I knew my destination was only half a mile away.

![The canyon carved by the South Fork Kern River (36°8’10.6058″ N 118°5’32.4522″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4286-1024x683.jpg)

![The final ascent from Dutch John Flat (36°7’49.5285″ N 118°6’41.9175″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/281A4290-1024x683.jpg)

I finally reached the campsite shortly before 7pm after almost 13 hours of hiking. It was a spot I had discovered during the previous hike. There were a few flat tent sites with a bench and a rock oven, and a few more signs that humans had used this as a campsite quite regularly. Just not since the 2008 Clover Fire that had burned the adjacent areas. I dropped my backpack and sat down for a few minutes. Then I boiled water for my [Stowaway Gourmet Chana Masala](https://www.trailspace.com/gear/stowaway-gourmet/chana-masala/). While it was rehydrating, I set up my tent. The sun was about to set and I thoroughly enjoyed the savory food while listening to a gentle wind brush through the canopy of the pine trees around me. Retreating into my tent, I fell asleep early, exhausted from a long day filled with great views but lots of ascent with little shade and a good amount of bushwhacking and trail finding.

---

**Trail Conditions:** Olancha Pass Trail is in decent condition. Several sections are a bit sandy and there are lots of loose rocks on the trail, but the trail is generally easy to hike. Honey Bee Trail however is difficult to follow and very heavily overgrown in most places. The trail has not seen any maintenance in a long time. Most of the trail is very exposed to the sun traversing sagebrush meadows with no shade. Haiwee Pass Trail is in even worse condition, extensively damaged by the 2008 Clover Fire, faint, hard to follow, and heavily choked with brush.

**Parking:** There is plenty of room for cars at the trailhead.

**Crowds:** Absolute solitude. Didn’t meet a single person for the entire day.

**Weather:** Sunny with a few scattered clouds in the morning. Partly cloudy with a light intermittent breeze in the afternoon. Low of 70°F / 21°C around 10:20am, southeast of Olancha Pass. High of 91°F / 33°C in the afternoon on Honey Bee Trail, north of Haiwee Pass Trail. Humidity between 43% in the morning and 21% in the afternoon.

**Land Manager & Permits:** South Sierra Wilderness. Inyo National Forest and Sequoia National Forest. Wilderness permit required.

**Overall Rating:** 3.1

Difficulty: Very Strenuous  
Scenery: Moderate  
Logistics: Very Easy
