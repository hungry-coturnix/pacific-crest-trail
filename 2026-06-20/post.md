# Canebrake Road to Sherman Pass Road

Jun 20, 2026 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Southern California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/southern-california/)

**Start:** [35.835761, -118.044031](https://maps.app.goo.gl/eqjYM9oyE84m6WhB6) (Canebrake Road)  
**End:** [36.023596, -118.134145](https://maps.app.goo.gl/qfz9hcwxgfokWgrD8) (Sherman Pass Road)

**Distance:** 21.8 mi (up to PCT mile 703.4, 26.5% completed)  
**Time:** 9 hours 54 minutes (start 5:44am, end 3:38pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 4,043 ft (low 5,559 ft – high 8,012 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 3,596 ft (start 5,559 ft – end 6,006 ft)

---

There are several ways to describe today. First and foremost, it was a day of milestones. I reached legendary Kennedy Meadows South which marks the official end of the desert section of the Pacific Crest Trail and the beginning of the High Sierra. And I passed the 700 mile marker on trail. Both are significant accomplishments that I am very proud of. But I also hit a different kind of milestone today, albeit not on purpose: This was the first day in my life that I burned more than 10,000 kcal. It was a very exhausting day with almost 14 hours of hiking and cycling. The heat near Kennedy Meadows was draining. But even more draining was the bike ride back with almost 2,000 ft / 600 m of elevation gain. Let me start from the beginning.

My plan was to spend the night at Chimney Creek Campground again. The car rides were getting longer again, the lack of roads across the Sierra Nevada forced me to take a long detour via Tehachapi. The drive north on US-395 was spectacularly scenic. Kennedy Meadows Road steeply pierced the eastern escarpment of the Sierra Nevada. Kennedy Meadows was beautifully nestled among the mountains. It was shortly after 6pm when I reached the PCT at Sherman Pass Road, just west of Kennedy Meadows and within 100 yards of the South Fork Kern River. I chained my bike to a nearby road sign that said “Wild and Scenic River”, then made my way back. Leaving the paved road, I turned right onto Canebrake Road which led me to Chimney Creek Campground from the northern side. This time, I followed the campground road a little further to see if there were any other nice campsites. But further east it was also windier, so I turned around and parked in the exact same spot as last time.

The night was quiet and peaceful. I got up at 5am. Sunrise was at 5:36am and because I only had to drive a few minutes, I got the earliest start yet for any PCT hike. Four thru hikers passed me while I was still gearing up. I caught up to two of them, Rachel and Tim from Minnesota, who still hadn’t received any trail names. We hiked together for a bit and they quizzed me about my unique section-hiking logistics. At about 2 miles, I let them forge ahead while I took a short spur trail to Fox Mill Spring to take photos for the PCTA. The spring was not far from the scattered debris of a multilevel barite mill which was in operation until the early 1950s, a remnant of an era when this region was heavily prospected for gold, barium, and tungsten. Back on the PCT, the trail continued its ascent, crossed an abandoned dirt road, then turned east with views of Chimney Peak’s double points. The hillside had dense juniper trees.

Rounding a nose, the vegetation changed abruptly. The area ahead had burned during the devastating Manter Fire in July 2000. The lightning-fueled blaze had consumed over 74,000 acres sweeping through more than 75% of Domeland Wilderness. While the slow-growing juniper trees will take many more decades to fully return, nature’s resilient fire-followers had stepped in to fill the void. Brilliant white Mojave Prickly Poppies, radiant yellow California Flannelbush, Western Tansymustard, and delicate Prickly Sandwort all bloomed in stunning abundance. Just before another dirt road, I reached the day’s high point at 8,012 ft / 2,442 m. Ahead of me was a long, sweeping descent towards Manter Creek. In the distance, I could already see the grand tapestry of Rockhouse Basin, defined by the stark granite domes that give Domeland Wilderness its name. I took my second break in the small shade of a California Flannelbush. It was pretty warm now and shade was scarce. The High Sierra silhouette stretched across the horizon.

Several miles later, I reached the mouth of Manter Creek Canyon with Rockhouse Basin unfolding in front of me, granite domes in the distance. The wild and scenic South Fork Kern River was somewhere in the middle of the basin. The trail turned north and crossed Manter Creek where I stopped to filter water. What first seemed like an idyllic, willow-shaded oasis quickly turned into a battlefield: the area was heavily plagued by aggressive deer flies. Their sharp, painful bites were quite annoying, and even insect repellent didn’t seem to help. I didn’t stay too long and pressed north along the eastern edge of Rockhouse Basin. The PCT was sandy at times, passing through some leftover forest of pinyon pines. Approaching a gateway of resistant metamorphic rock, the trail dropped down to the South Fork Kern River for the first time. Two women were swimming in the river and enjoying the refreshing water. They invited me to join them which I politely declined.

Further north, the trail headed through willows along the edge of the river, then veered northeast to cross Pine Creek and follow another bend in the river before heading back up to a sandy ridge with juniper trees. I could see an abandoned house ahead, just on the edge of Domeland Wilderness and Sequoia National Forest. The PCT paralleled a barbed wire fence and passed west of the house. At a fence corner, the trail angled to the northeast and climbed the western slope of another hill. On the other side was Kennedy Meadows, stretching out east of the river. The trail dropped back down to the river where I filtered water one more time. Passing through 3 gates, I finally reached Sherman Pass Road and returned to my bicycle. It was definitely hot now and the last few miles had been quite draining in the heat with very little shade. But I was also excited that I had made it to the end of the desert and was now at the gateway to the High Sierra.

**Trail Conditions:** Mostly well-maintained dirt singletrack with some patches of loose granitic scree and some sandy stretches along South Fork Kern River. The area a mile north of Fox Mill Spring all the way to Kennedy Meadows was severely impacted by the 2000 Manter Fire. Even though this fire was 26 years ago, there are still scarred trees along the trail. On a positive note, the fire gave a lot of room to California Flannelbush which is abundant in this stretch of the PCT.

**Parking:** There is room for three cars along Canebrake Road.

**Crowds:** Lightly traveled. Met 13 northbound thru hikers throughout the day.

**Weather:** Sunny with no clouds. Light to moderate intermittent breeze past noon. Low of 46°F / 8°C in the morning, at the beginning of the hike near Chimney Creek Campground. High of 82°F / 28°C in the early afternoon along South Fork Kern River, although the sun exposed areas along the river were much hotter than that. Humidity between 12% and 24%.

**Land Manager & Permits:** Chimney Peak Wilderness. Domeland Wilderness. Sequoia National Forest. No permit required.

**Overall Rating:** 3.5

Difficulty: Very Strenuous  
Scenery: Great  
Logistics: Difficult

---

**Bike Shuttle:** 18.4 mi (3 hours 32 minutes), 1,919 ft elevation gain, 5.3 mph average speed, 37.1 mph maximum speed

I knew the bike ride back was going to be exhausting, but I had still underestimated how grueling it would be. Fortunately, most of it was paved. After hopping into the saddle, it was immediately uphill. I settled on a slow but steady rhythm. Passing several thru hikers, I reached the famous Kennedy Meadows General Store which has a tradition of greeting every arriving hiker with loud cheers and pot banging. Just as I was passing by, another hiker had arrived and so I took the cheerful welcome as an appreciation for my own hiking milestone even though I knew it was meant for the other hiker.

Turning right on Kennedy Meadows Road, the ascent continued. Some stretches were almost flat, but some were also a bit steeper. My energy was dwindling and the radiating high-desert heat offered no favors. I passed Grumpy Bear’s Retreat, a very popular hostel for thru hikers taking a day off from hiking in Kennedy Meadows. A shuttle had just arrived with a bunch of thru hikers and the shuttle driver was turning around. She asked me whether I was okay. I explained my situation, but she was unfortunately on a schedule and couldn’t help me. I took a break for a few minutes and had more water, then continued. The incline was steeper now. With empty legs, I began alternating between short bursts of cycling and pushing my bike on foot. Two hours into the ride, I made the executive decision to try and hitchhike if a truck passed that could accommodate both me and my bike. It was still a few miles to the highest point and it felt like I might not get there. Only a few cars and RVs passed, none of them able to take a bicycle.

Fortunately, the final two miles leading up to the pass leveled out slightly, allowing me to get back on the pedals and ride to the summit. The relief was absolute. Ahead lay more than four miles of pure, glorious downhill. I tucked in and picked up incredible speed, cruising down the asphalt of Kennedy Meadows Road before turning onto the unpaved dirt of Canebrake Road. There were a few uphill stretches again and I ended up pushing my bike for a bit more here and there. The shadows were also growing longer and it was starting to get a bit chilly. I finally reached the last saddle after over 3 hours. Now it was really just downhill for another 2 miles back to my car. The dirt road was relatively easy to navigate and when I reached the car, I was utterly, completely spent but triumphant.
