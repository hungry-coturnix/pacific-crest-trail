# Trail Pass to Cottonwood Pass (Day 1)

Aug 22, 2026 — by [Tilman](https://pct.ahoh.de/author/tilman/) in [Central California](https://pct.ahoh.de/category/central-california/)

**Start:** [36.448336, -118.170638](https://maps.app.goo.gl/DmW1mNZnJkUwNtfk9) (Cottonwood Pass Trailhead)  
**End:** [36.438189, -118.312949](https://maps.app.goo.gl/fXqXo1GgLgqj36CK7) (Rocky Basin Lakes)

**Distance:** 18.2 mi (up to PCT mile 746.5, 28.2% completed)  
**Time:** 10 hours 7 minutes (start 6:18am, end 4:25pm)  
**Elevation Gain:** 2,940 ft (low 8,935 ft – high 10,580 ft)  
**Elevation Loss:** 2,296 ft (start 9,936 ft – end 10,580 ft)

---

I was back for another backpacking trip through Golden Trout Wilderness. Technically, this trip was only going to cover 5 miles of the Pacific Crest Trail and doable as a short day hike, but I had decided to turn it into a two day backpacking adventure to see a bit more of Golden Trout Wilderness. The route was going to take me back across Trail Pass towards Mulkey Meadows, but then further west to the headwaters of the South Fork Kern River and to the Rocky Basin Lakes where I would camp. On the second day, I would cross Big Whitney Meadow and hike up Cottonwood Pass, then take the Pacific Crest Trail south to close the loop at Trail Pass. This would get me within 10 miles of Mount Whitney. I did not win a permit in the lottery for Mount Whitney this year, so I would have to hike past it on my next trip.

Staying at Mount Whitney Motel in Lone Pine, I got up at 5:20am and drove up the long switchbacks of Horseshoe Meadow Road to Cottonwood Pass Trailhead. Just as on the previous trip, I scored a parking spot right opposite the trail. Sunrise was at 6:16am and I left the trailhead just a few minutes later as the sun was starting to illuminate the surrounding peaks. The initial stretch across Horseshoe Meadow and up the sandy switchbacks to Trail Pass was intimately familiar. Sweeping views opened up across the basin toward Mulkey Meadow below, with Olancha Peak towering prominently on the distant horizon – immediately bringing back vivid memories of the previous trip. After descending the southern side of Trail Pass, I stopped for my first break near Mulkey Meadows in the exact same spot as before. The scene this morning, however, featured a large herd of cattle moving across the golden meadow and lowing loudly, their calls echoing across the valley floor. Even within a designated wilderness, livestock grazing remains permitted here under grandfathered provisions in federal legislation.

![Tunnel Meadow (36°22’59.7297″ N 118°15’34.1123″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4811-1024x683.jpg)

I continued following Trail Pass Trail west instead of turning south along Mulkey Meadows. Crossing over a large sand bank just to the north, the trail cut through a dry, open wash with Trail Peak towering prominently on the right above the forested rim. Emerging onto the edge of Bullfrog Meadow, the open basin stretched out ahead, framed by a distinct unnamed conical peak to the southwest. Feeling a sudden dip in energy, I decided to take another quick break here after a mere 2 miles – resting against my backpack and dozing off for a few peaceful minutes in the sun. Back on trail, I left the meadow and headed up a low saddle, only to quickly drop back down into another valley holding the South Fork Kern River. I had crossed this river multiple times over the last few trips but this was as close to the headwaters as anyone could get on a trail, with its true source originating just a few miles to the north, below the Pacific Crest Trail. Here below Trail Peak, the river was reduced to a docile, shaded stream only a few feet wide and easily crossed without getting my shoes wet.

![The junction with Tunnel Cutoff Trail leading towards Big Whitney Meadow (36°22’36.6528″ N 118°16’27.5088″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4818-1024x683.jpg)

![Kern Peak in the distance from Tunnel Cutoff Trail (36°22’50.0232″ N 118°17’14.784″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4820-1024x683.jpg)

The trail stayed on the east side of the river for a short while, then crossed over to the western side. At the junction with McConnel Meadow Trail, I spotted three tents on the other side of the river. And someone had even strung a clothes lines to dry their gear. Further ahead was the beginning of Tunnel Meadow, a relatively narrow, sunlit meadow stretching southwest with the South Fork Kern River running down its center. About halfway through the meadow near the junction with Tunnel Cutoff Trail, prominent granite outcroppings rose sharply out of the flat meadow floor. A weathered wooden sign nailed to a tree marked the turnoff toward Big Whitney Meadow and Rocky Basin Lakes. Leaving the main path, I turned right to head over a saddle toward Golden Trout Creek.

The trail was hard to follow though. I had to use my offline map and best judgement to find a suitable path up the fairly steep terrain. Closer to the top of the saddle, a few cairns appeared and made navigation easier. On the way down through the sandy clearings, I spotted Kern Peak rising in the distance through a gap in the pine trees. Now on Siberian Pass Trail, I was back on proper trail tread. Just a short while later, I reached a creek crossing at Golden Trout Creek. A couple with their dog was taking a break nearby, the dog having a blast splashing in the water. I found a way to cross the creek a little further upstream, hopping across exposed boulders to keep my shoes dry. Knowing this might be the last reliable water source before camp, I took advantage of the stop to filter water.

![Along the crystal clear waters of Golden Trout Creek (36°24’13.284″ N 118°17’8.082″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4832-1024x683.jpg)

![A dry meadow along Barigan Stringer (36°25’12.141″ N 118°17’50.4138″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4844-1024x683.jpg)

Paralleling Golden Trout Creek, the trail climbed uphill again at a moderate incline, tracing right along the crystal clear water as it tumbled over granite rocks beneath the pine canopy. I took another break 2 miles later, already fairly exhausted from the day’s distance. It was warm, albeit not as hot as the last trip. Shortly before reaching Barigan Stringer, I turned left onto Rocky Basin Lakes Trail. The first half mile was even more steeply uphill. Initially climbing through pine forest, the trail started skirting and passing several smaller meadows, all of them dry with no flowing creek still connecting them. Out of the corner of my eyes, I saw movement ahead – I had roused a stag with particularly large antlers. It quickly disappeared behind a ridge. The last and northernmost meadow before the next trail junction was the largest. Surrounded by dense pine forest, it looked exceptionally welcoming. I took my last break here before the final push to Rocky Basin Lakes, with tiredness already seeping into my bones. After crossing the Little Whitney to Big Whitney Meadow Trail, the last ascent was demanding. Crossing dry Barigan Stringer once more, the trail switchbacked up to a small lake just before the basin. It was still 2 miles to the end of the trail and the larger upper lakes, but there was still more elevation gain ahead and I was exhausted. The little meadow and the serene subalpine lake were pleasant, so I decided to call it a day and make camp right here.

![The northernmost meadow along Rocky Basin Lakes Trail (36°25’25.932″ N 118°17’57.918″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4849-1024x683.jpg)

![The lake along Rocky Basin Lakes Trail where I decided to camp (36°26’17.5592″ N 118°18’46.5069″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/281A4855-1024x683.jpg)

![View from my tent shortly before sunset (36°26’17.6597″ N 118°18’46.8031″ W)](https://pct.ahoh.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/PXL_20260823_015151967-1024x768.jpg)

I put up my tent on the south side of the lake, on a level spot covered in small pine cones that I carefully moved out of the way. The lake was very shallow and scooping up enough water without stirring up too much dirt was difficult. I had to do it twice, and even then, the water had a light green color. My water filter took care of that though and the clean water tasted like any other water I had had. I sat on my bear canister in front of my tent and enjoyed the scenery. There were absolutely no mosquitoes. I was stunned. It was probably already too late in the summer. For dinner, I had [Farm to Summit Puebloan Beans and Rice](https://farmtosummit.com/products/puebloan-beans-and-rice). It was well spiced and tasty. I watched the shadows get longer and the sun set, then retreated into my tent for some well deserved sleep.

---

**Trail Conditions:** Trail Pass Trail is quite sandy, especially around Trail Pass and through Mulkey Meadows and Tunnel Meadow. Tunnel Cutoff Trail is hard to follow at times although there are a few cairns to guide the way. Siberian Pass Trail and Rocky Basin Lakes Trail are in good condition and easy to follow.

**Parking:** There is plenty of room for cars at the trailhead.

**Crowds:** Very lightly traveled. Only met 7 people and 1 dog throughout the day, all of them near Tunnel Meadow.

**Weather:** Sunny with a few scattered clouds throughout the entire day. Unfortunately, I lost all my weather recordings due to spotty cellphone service when I tried to upload the data.

**Land Manager & Permits:** Golden Trout Wilderness. Inyo National Forest. Wilderness permit required (no quota).

**Overall Rating:** 4.2

Difficulty: Strenuous  
Scenery: Great  
Logistics: Very Easy
