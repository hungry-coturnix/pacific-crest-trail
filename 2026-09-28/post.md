Cottonwood Pass to Bullfrog Lake Trail (Day 3)

Start: 36.730716, -118.373061 (Upper Bubbs Creek Canyon)
End: 36.772516, -118.341202 (Kearsarge Pass Trailhead)

Distance: 13.1 mi / 21.1 km (up to PCT mile 789.6, 29.8% completed)
Time: 6 hours 22 minutes (start 7:22am, end 1:45pm)
Elevation Gain: 2,647 ft / 807 m (low 9,176 ft / 2,797 m – high 11,814 ft / 3,601 m)
Elevation Loss: 4,007 ft / 1,221 m (start 10,536 ft / 3,211 m – end 9,176 ft / 2,797 m)

----------

It was the last day of my 3-day section hike through Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Parks. I woke up a few minutes before my alarm at 6:20am. It was no longer pitch dark, but it was still far from daylight. I thought back to the last two days and how privileged I had been to see some of the most stunning views in the Sierra Nevada. Today's hike would be shorter than on the previous two days. I would follow the Pacific Crest Trail to Vidette Meadow and then up the ridge towards Glen Pass. But I would leave the PCT early and turn onto Bullfrog Lake Trail, then make it up to Kearsarge Pass (11,814 ft / 3,601 m). From there it was 5 mi / 8 km downhill all the way to Onion Valley where Chris and I had staged my car three days earlier under the brilliant light of the full Harvest Moon. The 1-mile / 1.6-km stretch from Vidette Meadow to Bullfrog Lake Trail was going to be familiar; I had hiked it in 2018 as part of the Rae Lakes Loop. I was expecting the views to be just as stunning as the day before.

I crawled out of my sleeping bag. The water in the clean water bag was not frozen. I had expected temperatures to plummet again, but apparently they had not dropped below 36°F / 2°C during the night. Descending into tree cover had been a good idea. I looked up and there were some scattered clouds clinging to University Peak (13,632 ft / 4,155 m), which had glowed in the evening light during my descent yesterday, but overall the sky was clear. Breakfast was Stowaway Gourmet Berry Yogurt Parfait and a cup of hot Jasmine tea. The parfait was interesting; it had a separate pouch for powdered yogurt which you would then pour over some cereal. It was not bad, but it didn't quite taste like yogurt, and I will probably not get it again. I ate in my sleeping bag to stay warm, but then I had to pack up and get out into the cold morning air. I was ready to go by 7:22am and double-checked that I had everything with me.

The canyon was still in the shade; it would take a while for the sun to get over the Sierra crest and reach the canyon bottom. I crossed Bubbs Creek easily, hopping across granite boulders in the chilled mountain stream. In the decades after Visalia prospector and stockman John Bubb pioneered his rough 1864 pack trail over Kearsarge Pass into this upper canyon in search of grazing range and minerals, his route quickly became one of the earliest and most vital trans-Sierra pack highways, linking cattle ranches in the San Joaquin Valley with booming Owens Valley mining camps and hauling tons of freight across the crest long before modern roads existed. At an opening a quarter mile / 0.4 km later, I looked back south upcanyon across a quiet subalpine meadow strewn with deadfall and fringed by lodgepole pines. The striking granite pyramid of Center Peak (12,760 ft / 3,889 m) – named in 1864 by James T. Gardiner of the California Geological Survey for its symmetrical position dividing upper Bubbs Creek into Center Basin and the main canyon – still towered in deep morning shadow on the left, while early sunlight illuminated the high western ridge of Stanford Spur and the distant summit horn of Junction Peak (13,888 ft / 4,233 m) poking through drifting cloud wisps above the cirque headwall toward Forester Pass.

The next few miles passed quickly. It was gently downhill through tall Sierra lodgepole pines and granite talus with a bit of a rocky trail tread, but overall an easy hike. Soon enough, I could see the rounded granodiorite dome of Mount Bago (11,870 ft / 3,618 m) catching the bright morning sunlight in the distance where I would be ascending out of the canyon, while the waning gibbous daytime moon – the same Harvest Moon under which Chris and I had staged my car three nights earlier – set quietly over the glaciated trough of Bubbs Creek canyon.

[PHOTO: 281A6357.jpg][CAPTION: Mount Bago (11,870 ft / 3,618 m) catches bright morning sunlight ~2.4 mi / 3.9 km to the west-northwest as the daytime moon sets over the glaciated trough of Bubbs Creek canyon. Tall Sierra lodgepole pines and granite talus frame the quiet subalpine descent toward Vidette Meadow. (36°45'19.07" N 118°23'53.10" W)]

As I approached Vidette Meadow in the cool morning shadows cast by the towering Kearsarge Pinnacles to the north, I spotted my first mule deer (*Odocoileus hemionus*) of the day browsing quietly near the edge of the trees. After about 3 mi / 4.8 km, near Vidette Meadow, I stopped for my first break. The meadow and its rushing stream take their name from East and West Vidette, christened in 1898 by Cornelius Beach Bradley – the same UC Berkeley English professor and Sierra Club explorer honored by Mount Bradley above Center Basin – from the military term *vedette*, a mounted sentinel stationed ahead of an outpost to watch for an enemy, because the twin pyramid-like peaks stand like sentinels guarding the valley. I sat down right next to Bubbs Creek and watched the morning sun, which had now crested the ridge, glisten on the crystal-clear cascades rushing over granitic river stones between weathered lodgepole pine roots and autumn-tinted willows.

[PHOTO: 281A6361.jpg][CAPTION: Morning sunlight illuminates the crystal-clear cascades of Bubbs Creek as it rushes over granitic river stones near Vidette Meadow. Weathered lodgepole pine roots anchor the forested southern bank, while autumn-tinted willows fringe the water's edge. (36°45'33.50" N 118°24'28.26" W)]

With a fresh bottle of filtered water, I continued. Soon enough, I reached the junction where the trail toward Junction Meadow and Bubbs Creek continued west, but the Pacific Crest Trail turned north. There was a handwritten note from a ranger attached to the guidepost warning about bears that had been stealing food from hikers and had become quite accustomed to humans. It encouraged hikers to chase bears away and emphasized: "Get mad. Be mean. Be the bigger bear." That put a smile on my face.

Past the junction, the ascent was very steep. It immediately reminded me of the last stretch up Forester Pass the previous day. The trail switchbacked aggressively and gained elevation quickly. Through a break in the lodgepole pines past a map-lichen-crusted granodiorite boulder, the sheer, avalanche-scoured northern face of East Vidette (12,356 ft / 3,766 m) towered dramatically above Bubbs Creek canyon.

Glaciated granitic slabs and sheer avalanche chutes define its dramatic northern face as morning sunlight illuminates the upper basin. (36°45'42.56" N 118°24'38.98" W)]

Just a minute higher up the switchbacks, where sunlit greenleaf manzanita (*Arctostaphylos patula*) anchored the weathered boulders in the foreground, the angle opened southeast up the canyon gap to reveal Center Peak (12,760 ft / 3,889 m) rising prominently at the head of the valley behind East Vidette.

Climbing another 400 ft / 122 m up the switchbacks, the view south expanded to show West Vidette (12,520 ft / 3,816 m) flanking the deep canyon opposite East Vidette and framing the craggy double-horned summit of Deerhorn Mountain (13,265 ft / 4,043 m) in the distant center – named in 1895 by Joseph N. LeConte for its pair of sharp summit pinnacles that mimic the antlers of a buck – while golden autumn willow thickets glowed across the basin floor below.

As I ascended another longer switchback, I met two backpackers coming down. We chatted for a bit. They were doing the Rae Lakes Loop like I had in 2018. They asked me about my previous days and when I told them my mileages, they reverently called me a "beast" and considered me very fit. I didn't quite feel like I deserved that compliment. Just a little further, I noticed two more mule deer (*Odocoileus hemionus*) among the granite boulders – an adult doe and her alert fawn, bringing my morning tally to three. The doe was outfitted with a bright orange neck collar for VHF and GPS tracking as part of the California Department of Fish and Wildlife's long-term study of the Round Valley mule deer herd. These deer undertake one of the most arduous seasonal migrations in the state, climbing from winter sagebrush scrub in the Owens Valley over the 11,814 ft / 3,601 m crest of Kearsarge Pass into Kings Canyon's lush subalpine meadows for summer forage before racing back east ahead of autumn snows.

A few minutes higher at 10,236 ft / 3,120 m, where hardy foxtail pines clung to the sun-drenched granitic talus above the forested floor of Bubbs Creek canyon, a sweeping southward panorama opened up across the entire drainage. Center Peak, East Vidette, Deerhorn Mountain, and West Vidette lined the horizon, while the sharp pyramidal horn of Mount Brewer (13,481 ft / 4,109 m) now appeared on the far right skyline crowning the Great Western Divide.

In the foreground, hardy foxtail pines cling to the sun-drenched granitic talus above the forested floor of Bubbs Creek canyon. (36°45'57.40" N 118°24'30.29" W)]

When I finally reached the junction with Bullfrog Lake Trail at 10,572 ft / 3,222 m, I was elated that the ascent would ease for a bit as the footpath leveled out onto an open subalpine bench. Directly ahead to the north-northeast across a golden late-season meadow rose the granodiorite pyramid of Mount Rixford (12,887 ft / 3,928 m), named in 1899 by Joseph N. LeConte in honor of San Francisco surgeon and mountaineer Dr. Emmet Rixford, who had completed the peak's first recorded ascent two years earlier. Turning onto the side trail, I was sad to leave the PCT again, and I made a promise to come back soon to continue north toward Glen Pass.

The trail soon passed a calm, unnamed subalpine tarn (~10,560 ft / 3,219 m) nestled in a golden sedge meadow fringed by foxtail and lodgepole pines, its mirror-like surface reflecting the soft autumn sky and the craggy southern crest of Mount Rixford.

A short stroll further brought me to the much bigger Bullfrog Lake (10,634 ft / 3,241 m). Both lakes were incredibly beautiful, with Bullfrog's cobalt and sapphire waters reflecting the surrounding granite crags as fresh cumulus clouds began gathering to the east above the saddle of Kearsarge Pass (11,814 ft / 3,601 m) and the rugged pyramid of Peak 12,275 ft / 3,741 m on the Sierra Crest.

[PHOTO: 281A6411.jpg][CAPTION: Subalpine foxtail pines frame the cobalt-blue waters of Bullfrog Lake beneath Kearsarge Pass (11,814 ft / 3,601 m) and the rugged granodiorite pyramid of Peak 12,275 ft / 3,741 m on the Sierra Crest. Fresh cumulus clouds begin gathering along the eastern divide ahead of an incoming autumn snowstorm. (36°46'22.36" N 118°24'25.22" W)]

John Muir had originally called it Bryanthus Lake in 1899, but local prospectors nicknamed it Bullfrog Lake after the abundant native mountain yellow-legged frogs (*Rana sierrae*) that once inhabited its shallows. In the early and mid-twentieth century, the lake's accessible location and sublime setting made it so popular with campers and commercial pack stock that its shoreline suffered severe ecological degradation. In 1961, the National Park Service enacted an emergency, permanent closure prohibiting all camping and grazing within the basin – one of the earliest wilderness restoration closures in national park history. Today, the fragile subalpine turf and clean waters have made a remarkable recovery. Pointing my camera south across the shimmering sapphire water from the northern shore offered spectacular scenery with East Vidette (12,356 ft / 3,766 m), Deerhorn Mountain (13,265 ft / 4,043 m), and West Vidette (12,520 ft / 3,816 m) rising above the granodiorite ledges.

(36°46'23.37" N 118°24'14.14" W)]

Walking along the northwest shore, I noticed that the cumulus clouds gathering to the southeast above University Peak (13,632 ft / 4,155 m) and the rugged granite slopes of the Kearsarge Pinnacles (12,042 ft / 3,670 m) were growing thicker over the rippling water.

Near the eastern end of the lake, one last look back southwest across the vibrant water framed the sharp pyramidal horn of Mount Brewer (13,481 ft / 4,109 m) in the distant notch between Deerhorn Mountain and West Vidette on the left and the eastern granitic shoulder of Mount Bago on the right. Named for William Henry Brewer, principal assistant of the California Geological Survey, Mount Brewer was famously summited in July 1864 by Clarence King and Richard Cotter right before they launched their daring cross-country trek to Mount Tyndall – the peak I had admired yesterday on my climb toward Forester Pass.

As I left Bullfrog Lake behind, the clouds up ahead around the northern side of University Peak and Kearsarge Pass were getting darker by the minute. There were still a few patches of blue sky, but it was starting to worry me a little. Past the lake, the trail started its relentless ascent east-southeast towards Kearsarge Pass. I still had more than 1,000 ft / 305 m of elevation gain ahead of me as the footpath wound through an open, resilient woodland of cinnamon-barked Sierra foxtail pines (*Pinus balfouriana* subsp. *austrina*) – the same ancient conifers that had accompanied me since Cottonwood Pass on Day 1 and reach the northern terminus of their southern Sierra range just a few basins north of here.

The sun was still peeking through every now and then with great views back west to the lake I had come from. Directly behind Bullfrog Lake, Mount Bago's (11,870 ft / 3,618 m) distinctive reddish-brown summit dome stood out in striking contrast against the surrounding sea of pale gray granodiorite. The mountain's cap is an ancient metamorphic roof pendant – the baked, oxidized remnant of pre-batholithic volcanic and sedimentary rock that formed the ceiling of the subterranean magma chamber over 100 million years ago, remarkably surviving millions of years of tectonic uplift and violent glacial scour.

Puffy autumn cumulus clouds gather overhead as the trail climbs east toward Kearsarge Pass (11,814 ft / 3,601 m). (36°46'15.15" N 118°23'20.28" W)]

But up ahead, the clouds cloaking the jagged granodiorite arêtes and talus cirques along the Sierra Crest were getting darker and darker. And then it started snowing. Just a few snowflakes that didn't stick to the ground, but definitely snow. I immediately thought back to the couple near Wallace Creek yesterday who had warned me of snow forecasted above 8,500 ft / 2,591 m; the storm was arriving several hours earlier than I had counted on, and now I was getting worried about what would await me on the pass and on the other side.

Looking back west from 11,139 ft / 3,395 m across golden-amber subalpine willows, the turquoise oval of Bullfrog Lake still gleamed in its granite bowl beneath Mount Bago's ochre-capped summit, while farther west-southwest across Bubbs Creek canyon the rugged crests of Cross Mountain (12,122 ft / 3,695 m) and Mount Farquhar (12,752 ft / 3,887 m) – named for mountaineer and Sierra Club historian Francis P. Farquhar – pierced the distant horizon beneath building afternoon clouds.

On the upside, climbing the switchbacks at 11,186 ft / 3,409 m gave me a great view south across the stepped azure basin of the Kearsarge Lakes (~10,800 ft / 3,292 m – 10,950 ft / 3,338 m), nestled directly beneath the jagged granite spires of the Kearsarge Pinnacles (12,042 ft / 3,670 m), although the water looked dark and brooding without direct sunlight. As I had noted when glimpsing the Pinnacles yesterday, the pass, lakes, and spires owe their name to a fierce 1864 Civil War rivalry in the Owens Valley: after Confederate sympathizers named the Alabama Hills for the commerce raider *CSS Alabama*, Union miners proudly named their high-altitude claims and this pass after the Union sloop-of-war *USS Kearsarge*, which had sunk the *Alabama* off the French coast that June. Those same Union miners soon founded Kearsarge City at ~10,500 ft / 3,200 m in the high eastern canyon below the pass to exploit rich silver-lead veins. But the extreme altitude proved unforgiving: in early March 1867, a catastrophic winter storm unleashed an enormous avalanche that swept down the granite cirque, crushing the mining camp and mill and ending one of the highest boomtowns in California history.

A few backpackers were coming down from the pass and I asked them what conditions were on the other side. Their response – pretty much the same, but windier – did not lift my spirits. The last few switchbacks to Kearsarge Pass were hard. It was steep and cold and my strength was not what it had been on the first day, as I followed another lone backpacker grinding up the rocky tread ahead of me beneath the storm-draped crest.

Below a solo backpacker ascending the final talus switchbacks, all four stepped tarns of the Kearsarge Lakes sat cradled beneath the sheer spires of Kearsarge Pinnacles, with Bullfrog Lake resting on its forested bench below Mount Bago in the midground and the distant horn of Mount Brewer piercing the western horizon.

Back above the tree line, there was nothing to shield me from the wind, so I decided to push over the pass as fast as possible and then descend quickly to get back to tree cover. When I reached the pass, which marks the boundary between Kings Canyon National Park and Inyo National Forest's John Muir Wilderness, there were also two day hikers who had made it up there and were just turning around. The eastern side was indeed not blue sky, but dark, gloomy clouds. Standing at 11,814 ft / 3,601 m, I was perched at the lip of one of the steepest and most dramatic topographic fault scarps on earth – the Great Sierra Escarpment, which plunges nearly 8,000 ft / 2,438 m of sheer vertical relief over just 10 mi / 16 km down to the desert floor of Owens Valley (~3,900 ft / 1,189 m). The visual contrast was staggering: while bitter freezing wind and drifting snowflakes lashed the crest, bright, golden sunlight bathed the Great Basin desert miles below, an unforgettable living demonstration of the Sierra Nevada's fierce orographic barrier and desert rain shadow. From the pass, I could also look down into the deep eastern cirque holding Big Pothole Lake (11,300 ft / 3,444 m), a classic glacial tarn deeply scoured into the solid granodiorite bench just below the crest, with Independence Peak (11,631 ft / 3,545 m) rising beyond the canyon.

[PHOTO: 281A6488.jpg][CAPTION: Looking east-southeast from Kearsarge Pass (11,814 ft / 3,601 m) into the deep glacial cirque holding Big Pothole Lake (11,300 ft / 3,444 m), with Independence Peak (11,631 ft / 3,545 m) rising beyond the canyon and the sunlit expanse of Owens Valley (~3,900 ft / 1,189 m) visible in the distant haze beneath gathering storm clouds. (36°46'23.71" N 118°22'35.02" W)]

I pushed forward, descending as quickly as I could. Descending from the cirque holding Big Pothole Lake, I tackled numerous short switchbacks whose sweeping views were shrouded in doomsday clouds. Soon Heart Lake (~10,860 ft / 3,310 m) appeared on my right, unmistakably shaped like a heart. The chain of sparkling tarns nestled along Independence Creek – Big Pothole Lake (11,300 ft / 3,444 m), Heart Lake (~10,860 ft / 3,310 m), Flower Lake (10,540 ft / 3,213 m), and Gilbert Lake (10,417 ft / 3,175 m) – forms a classic textbook *paternoster lake staircase*. During the Pleistocene Tioga glaciation (~20,000 – 15,000 years ago), a massive alpine glacier carved its way down the eastern fault scarp, plucking deep bedrock basins into stepped granodiorite benches that now resemble beads strung along a rosary. Back below the tree line, I took a short break for some water and a snack. But I didn't stay long. I was soaked in sweat from the inside and it was cold and windy. I didn't feel cold, but I certainly didn't want my body to cool off. It was still snowing very lightly, but not sticking to the ground. I was descending further past Flower Lake, when I suddenly saw a bright flash, followed 3 seconds later by roaring thunder. I didn't think these kinds of clouds would bear lightning and thunder, but now I was even more scared. Being exposed on a mountain when there is a chance of lightning is never a good idea. I had to get back down as quickly as possible. I hurried past Gilbert Lake (10,417 ft / 3,175 m) – named in honor of Grove Karl Gilbert (1843 – 1918), one of the founding giants of American geology and a pioneer in understanding the glacial sculpting and tectonic faulting of the Sierra Nevada – where golden autumn willows glowed along the shoreline beneath the brooding storm clouds and the crags of Independence Peak.

Moments later, the light snow turned into graupel with a fierce shower battering the ground and accumulating in crisp white pellets between the granodiorite boulders and foxtail pine duff. Graupel forms through riming, when supercooled cloud droplets freeze instantly upon contact with falling snowflakes, creating dense, pellet-like soft hail.

[PHOTO: 281A6510.jpg][CAPTION: Freshly fallen graupel pellets accumulate between granodiorite boulders and pine duff along the Kearsarge Pass Trail below Gilbert Lake (10,417 ft / 3,175 m). The crisp white pellets mark the onset of an early autumn High Sierra thundersnow squall descending over Onion Valley (9,176 ft / 2,797 m). (36°46'14.84" N 118°21'8.38" W)]

I passed a few more day hikers who had wisely turned around as dark storm clouds and sweeping snow flurries engulfed the jagged north face and cirque of University Peak (13,632 ft / 4,155 m) towering to the south-southwest above Onion Valley.

[PHOTO: 281A6513.jpg][CAPTION: Dark autumn storm clouds engulf the jagged north face and cirque of University Peak (13,632 ft / 4,155 m) as early snow flurries sweep across the subalpine foxtail pine ridges above Onion Valley (9,176 ft / 2,797 m). (36°46'14.28" N 118°21'6.77" W)]

Late September in the High Sierra often marks this violent meteorological collision: when a cold arctic trough sweeps in over sun-warmed mountain terrain, fierce convective instability triggers rare high-altitude "thundersnow." Within a few minutes, the ground was almost solidly covered in white. This was really a snowstorm now! The wind was pushing the graupel against the mountain, fiercely and relentlessly. By now I had covered my camera with a rain cover for protection and was almost running down the mountain. More lightning and thunder erupted, making me twitch every time. The parking lot seemed so close, yet it was so far. The switchbacks treacherously hid the true distance I had to cover. As I was descending and the air getting a bit warmer, the graupel turned into very wet snow pellets, sticking to my jacket and my hat. The firmer pellets earlier had just bounced off me, keeping me mostly dry, but now I was also slowly getting soaked. During the last half mile / 0.8 km to the car, descending through the subalpine canyon of Onion Valley (9,176 ft / 2,797 m) – named by 1860s prospectors for the pungent wild Sierra onions (*Allium* spp.) that carpeted these moist meadow slopes – I worked out in my head the exact sequence of actions once I got to the car. Put the backpack on the back seat. Jump into the driver's seat. Take the wind jacket and hat off, put the camera on the floor. Then drive down the mountain and stop somewhere on the desert floor where it was not snowing or raining. What I hadn't accounted for was the snow on my backpack and on my hat.

When I finally reached the car, it was covered in a blanket of wet graupel, but fortunately, the road seemed to be just wet and otherwise fine. When I took off my backpack, I noticed that it looked just like the car. I tried to get the graupel off quickly, but at this point I also didn't care much anymore about getting it inside the car. When I sat in the driver's seat and took off my hat, more snow fell off and onto the center console. It was fine by me. I was incredibly relieved that I had made it off the mountain and out of this snowstorm. The road was fine; I didn't have to worry about snow chains, and I just had to drive down the mountain now. I sped down the switchbacks and then stopped a couple of miles / 3 km outside of Independence, finally away from snow and rain. I looked back to the Sierra and the dark dome of clouds clinging to the mountains. Later on, I read that this was the first snow of the season. On my drive back home, I could see that most of the eastern Sierra peaks were dusted in snow now. This last day had really been a rollercoaster. It had started out with blue sky and amazing views, then turned into a frightening snowstorm by noon with lightning and thunder. I was very glad I was safely back on the road; this day had really been one of a kind.

Trail Conditions: The Pacific Crest Trail, Bullfrog Lake Trail, and Kearsarge Pass Trail were all in good condition and easy to follow.

Parking: There is plenty of room for cars at the trailhead.

Crowds: Lightly traveled. Only met 24 people throughout the day, some of them day hikers.

Wildlife: A few ground squirrels and three mule deer.

Weather: Mostly sunny in the morning with a few clouds around the peaks. Increasing cloud cover towards Kearsarge Pass. Snowstorm with graupel, thunder, and lightning from Kearsarge Pass all the way to Onion Valley. Low of 35°F / 2°C around Kearsarge Pass. High of 62°F / 17°C along the switchbacks north of Vidette Meadow.

Land Manager & Permits: Kings Canyon National Park and Inyo National Forest, mostly in John Muir Wilderness. Wilderness permit required (no quota).
